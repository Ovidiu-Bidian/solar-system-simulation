This simulation is for scientific and educational purposes. It is also a support software for the book "Marea greșeală a „marelui” Einstein" by Ovidiu Bidian (Artpress, ISBN 978-973-108-587-6).

What is real in this simulation:

  • the motion of the Sun through absolute space, as science sees it today (2026): the Sun moves around the center of our galaxy, the Milky Way, on a huge orbit with a radius of about 26 000 light years, at about 230 km/s, one turn in about 230 million years. It moves towards the constellation Cygnus, at about 60° to the plane of the planets' orbits (the ecliptic), so the trails leave that plane at this angle. The absolute space of this simulation is a cylinder around the Sun's path. In this simulation the time frame of absolute space is about 165 years (one revolution of Neptune: the trails need that long to reach the wall of the space), so the path of the Sun through absolute space is a straight line. The trajectories of the celestial bodies through absolute space are represented by their yellow trails;
  • the orbits of the celestial bodies (Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune, Pluto) around the Sun: real orbital elements (JPL, epoch J2000), real periods, real sizes of the celestial bodies;
  • the orbit of the Moon around the Earth: the mean lunar elements (the orbit turns: its node once in 18.6 years, its perigee once in 8.85 years) and the main disturbances by the Sun (Meeus, from ELP-2000/82), within about 2 arc minutes (some 200 km) of its real place; the Moon always shows the same face to the Earth;
  • the motion of the planets on their orbits obeys Kepler's laws;
  • the rotation axes of the celestial bodies: real directions in space (for example Pluto's axis is tilted about 120° from the axis of its orbit, so Pluto spins backwards); the views around a celestial body use its real equator and rotation poles;
  • the surfaces of the celestial bodies: real maps (Pluto: NASA / JHUAPL / SwRI, New Horizons; Mercury, Venus (its clouds), Earth, the Moon, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn and its main rings, Uranus, Neptune: Solar System Scope);
  • the light: the celestial bodies are lit by the Sun, from the center of the Sun; the Moon's shadow on the Earth (eclipses of the Sun) and the Earth's shadow on the Moon (eclipses of the Moon), with umbra and penumbra; in the Earth's shadow the Moon is copper red, lit by the sunlight that the Earth's atmosphere bends into it.
  • the constellations: the real directions, brightness and colours of their stars (Hipparcos catalogue, J2000, through d3-celestial by Olaf Frohn), with their names. Among them those from which the motion of the Sun was found: Hercules (the solar apex, found by Herschel in 1783 near lambda Herculis from the motions of Sirius, Castor, Pollux, Procyon, Regulus, Arcturus and Altair), Lyra (Vega, near the apex), Columba (the antapex), Cygnus (the direction of the Sun around the center of the galaxy), Vela (the opposite direction) and Sagittarius (the center of the galaxy, whose motion on the sky gives the speed of the Sun, about 230 km/s).
  • the Milky Way: the glow of our galaxy's disk, as seen from the Earth on a dark night, from a real map (Deep Star Maps 2020, NASA Goddard Space Flight Center, Scientific Visualization Studio, from the Hipparcos, Tycho and Gaia catalogues), in its real place on the sky: brightest towards Sagittarius (the center of the galaxy), with its dark dust lanes; it is dimmed, so that the constellations remain easy to read.
  • the pan / tilt in the information panel: the direction of the view (the center of the screen) in the ecliptic coordinates of absolute space (J2000): pan is the ecliptic longitude, from the +x axis (the J2000 equinox), tilt the ecliptic latitude (+90 degrees = +z, the ecliptic's north pole), and "turn" the picture's turn from level to the ecliptic. They are the same in every view, also on the celestial bodies. What changes with the view is what the picture is kept level to, and so where the look up and down stops: in absolute space and in the Sun, Moon and Earth locked views at the ecliptic's poles (tilt +/-90 degrees); in the pole view's leveled pan at the body's poles, on its rotation axis (for the Earth Polaris and Sigma Octantis: tilt about +/-66.6 degrees); in the tilted meridian view and in the pole view's polar pan 90 degrees from the view on the body's center. On the Earth, in the pole view's leveled pan, within 30 Earth radii, the right ascension and declination are shown instead (below).
  • the right ascension and declination shown near the Earth (in the leveled pan of the pole view): the direction of the view in the J2000 equatorial frame, so the stars show their catalogue coordinates (the shift of a star's direction from a few Earth radii away is far below what any instrument can measure); for the Sun, the Moon and the planets it is their direction as seen from the camera (topocentric), not the geocentric values of the almanacs (from the center of the Earth): for the Moon they differ by up to about 1 degree at the Earth's surface and up to about 30 degrees at 30 Earth radii. The button next to "RA / Dec" switches between "J2000" and "adj. J2000": the J2000 coordinates adjusted to today's date (read from the computer's clock; the date is shown in the button's tooltip): the Earth's axis turns slowly (precession, one turn in about 26 000 years), so the coordinates of date differ from the catalogue's J2000 ones (axes of 1 January 2000): in 2026 by about 1.3 minutes of right ascension for most stars and up to 8 arc minutes of declination, much more near the poles (Polaris about 35 minutes of right ascension). They are the mean coordinates of date (IAU precession); the small nutation of the axis (up to 17 arc seconds), the aberration of light (up to 20.5 arc seconds, see below) and the stars' own motions, which the almanacs add for the apparent place, are not included: together they can change the last digit of the declination shown.
  • the stellar aberration (found by James Bradley in 1728): the Earth moves across the light of the stars (about 30 km/s on its orbit), so every star is seen shifted towards the direction the Earth moves, by up to 20.5 arc seconds (as an umbrella is tilted forward when walking in vertical rain). Only the Earth's motion on its orbit counts: the Sun's motion around the galaxy gives a shift that has stayed the same for millions of years and is already in the catalogue places. Under the RA / Dec row, the "Stellar ab." row shows it magnified (its button: off, ×62.5, ×125, ×187.5, ×250): all the stars, the constellation lines, their names and the Milky Way move, once a year, each star on its own small path: a short line near the ecliptic, a circle near its poles, an ellipse in between. The row shows the shift at the view direction (ΔRA / ΔDec); while it is on, the RA / Dec row shows the catalogue place of what is seen there, and the two rows added give the view direction. Bradley measured only the declination; the stars' parallax, much smaller, is not shown.
  • the stellar parallax and the proper motion of 16 near stars (in the table below), shown magnified under the "Stellar ab." row with the "Parallax" button (off, ×4000 ... ×20 000; the same for both, so that the shape of the path is true). The parallax (found by Bessel in 1838): the star is seen from the Earth, not from the Sun, so once a year it goes around a small ellipse (a short line near the ecliptic, a circle near its poles). The proper motion (found by Halley in 1718): the star's own motion across the sky, relative to the Sun, a straight drift. Together they draw the star's real path on the sky: loops, or a flat wave when the star moves more in a year than its ellipse is wide. The stars within 10° of the little cross get a yellow trail (their last 3 years), the line of their proper motion and a dot where the trail crosses that line (about twice a year). All the other stars stay at their catalogue places: the background against which these move. The rows show, for the star nearest the cross, its parallax shift and its proper motion in a year (ΔRA / ΔDec), then its catalogue parallax and, in brackets, the Copernican theory supporters' parallax (not real, see below: the star's shift measured only against the background stars, the proper motion not taken out, between the Earth at +x-ecl and, 178.8 days later, at −x-ecl). What is not real in this simulation:

  • the background stars: placed at random, with random brightness (the stars of the constellations are real);
  • the look of the Sun: its surface (granules, sunspots, faculae) and its explosions (flares, prominences, coronal mass ejections) are drawn by the program, much faster than in reality (in screen time, as the spin of the celestial bodies); they show what the Sun looks like, not what it does on a given day. The prominences are pink as seen through a hydrogen-alpha filter (in white light they are seen only in a total eclipse);
  • the spin of the celestial bodies: every celestial body turns once every 10 seconds, at every simulation speed (Pluto really turns once in about 6.4 Earth days);
  • the apparent size of the celestial bodies: a minimum apparent radius is set, so that a celestial body can be seen also from far away;
  • the brightness of the Moon in a total eclipse: shown as the eye sees it, adapted to the dark (in reality it is some 10 000 times fainter than the full Moon);
  • the black umbra of a total eclipse of the Sun on the Earth: drawn twice its real size (radius), so that it can be seen; in reality it is only about 1 - 4% of the radius of the whole dark spot (the penumbra), some 100 - 270 km wide; only when the real umbra reaches the Earth (otherwise only the penumbra is seen);
  • the dark side of the celestial bodies: lit a little, as the Earth's night side by a full Moon, so that its surface can be seen;
  • the Moon's trail: its real path is a spiral around the Earth's trail so stretched (one loop about 3.6 AU long and 0.0026 AU wide) that it would be only a line beside it; it is drawn as an eighth of a loop of a compressed spiral, from the Moon shrinking to the Earth's trail 100 Earth-Moon distances behind it, seen near the Earth and through the zoom screen (its directions around the Earth stay real);
  • in the Moon locked view the Moon is drawn as large as the Earth, and in the Earth locked view the Earth as small as the Moon, so that the Moon's phases and the Moon's face turned to the Earth can be seen (their places, paths, light and shadows stay real);
  • the shape of the celestial bodies: drawn as spheres with their mean radius (Saturn is about 10% and Jupiter about 6% flatter at the poles); the faint rings of Jupiter, Uranus and Neptune are not drawn;
  • the celestial bodies move on independent orbits (they do not attract each other); only the Sun, the planets, Pluto and the Moon are shown (no other moons, no asteroids or comets).

This simulation also draws the motions of stars, as stated above.

But first, a little history.
In old times (before 1500) people, with their imprecise instruments, saw the stars fixed on the sky; only the Sun, the Moon and the planets moved, around the Earth. This model is called the "geocentric model".
It was first challenged with a complete model by Copernicus (1543), who held that the Sun is fixed and the planets, the Earth among them, turn around it (this is also called the "heliocentric" or Copernican model). Later (Giordano Bruno, 1584) came the idea that the Sun is just a fixed star like the others seen on the sky, not "the center of the Universe".
Heliocentrism caused a huge agitation: it was seen as a direct challenge to the Church (to faith), because the Church held that the Earth is the center of the Universe. This theory was seen as a victory of science over faith (this is wrong in principle, because faith in God does not imply believing that the Earth is fixed or not, but this is another story, we will not go into details here).

The Copernican theory was later confirmed by observations with the telescope (Galileo, 1610: Venus turns around the Sun).
But later Herschel (1783) discovered that in fact the Sun moves too. This discovery did not cause the same agitation as the Copernican theory; in fact it was very "silent". The Copernican (heliocentric) model was not officially dismissed as wrong.
More than that, when Bessel (1838) discovered the stellar parallax, something that needs attention happened.
Since the heliocentric theory had not been clearly dismissed as a false theory contradicted by observations, its supporters said that this parallax was another proof that the Earth turns around a fixed star (the Sun). In the Copernican theory the parallax is a change of the position (angle) of a near fixed star against a background of other, very distant, fixed stars. The near star's motion is only apparent, due to the Earth's motion around the fixed Sun. The observation period is 6 months, and according to the Copernican theory the star should always move against the fixed background in the direction opposite to the Earth's motion around the Sun. In this simulation, with the Earth going from +x-ecl to −x-ecl, the star should always move towards +x-ecl. This is called "positive parallax" and it is the only one possible in the heliocentric (Copernican) model. Note that in the heliocentric model there is no "proper motion" of the stars: all the stars (the Sun, one of them, included) are fixed.
By chance, Bessel measured a star with a "positive parallax", but he (like Herschel) knew that the stars have proper motion: he knew that the stars move on the sky, that they are not fixed. Bessel's parallax is the star's shift with its proper motion taken out (the whole shift minus the proper motion), not the whole shift (the "Copernican" angle). Nevertheless the Copernican supporters "ignored" this "little detail" and said that what Bessel saw was another confirmation of the heliocentric theory.
With the instruments of Bessel's time only a few stars had a shift large enough to be measured, and about a third of those had what is called a "negative parallax" (the star moves in the same direction as the Earth): of all the Hipparcos stars, read with the Copernican definition, 0.8% shift more than 0.1″ (the precision of the 1830s) with a positive parallax and 0.4% with a negative one. If Bessel had measured such a star, this would have caused huge problems for the Copernican theory. But he didn't (this is how God sometimes leads people into temptation, to test their faith).

Many years later a space mission, Hipparcos (1989 - 1993), measured about 118 000 stars. Read with the Copernican definition, 12% of them show a "negative parallax" (55% a positive one, the rest too small to be measured), but science still did not "officially" dismiss the heliocentric theory, proven wrong. Some of these stars are also shown in this simulation.

The Copernican theory was never "officially" dismissed by science, and in my view this is the main reason why a lot of people today, more than 200 years after it was discovered that the Sun moves too, still think that the Sun is fixed and the Earth turns around it.

In reality, what turns around what depends on the reference system used. This simulation gives you the opportunity to study / explore / see this in detail. You can choose as the reference system absolute space (where everything moves), the Earth (where everything moves around the Earth), Saturn (where everything moves around Saturn), etc.

And no, this simulation is not a "conspiracy theory": this is what today's science (2026) tells us about the motion of the planets and the stars (the Sun is a star) in (absolute) space.
Science doesn't clearly state "what is true and what is wrong", and this contributes a lot to the confusion in society. Old, false theories that were proven wrong are not publicly declared wrong. And this is a huge problem of science.
