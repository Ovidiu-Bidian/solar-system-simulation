REQ-01
This requirement describes the general functionality of this simulation.

The purpose of the simulation is to present the motion of some celestial bodies of our solar system (the Sun, Mercury, Venus, the Earth, the Earth's satellite the Moon, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune and Pluto) through absolute space. According to current data, the Sun describes a circle with an enormous radius. For this simulation only a period of approximately 2000 years is considered, so the Sun describes a straight segment.

The absolute space used in this simulation is a parallelepiped with a length of 100 000 AU, a width of 100 AU (slightly larger than Pluto's orbit) and a height of 40 AU. The camera that observes the motion of the celestial bodies can be moved only inside this parallelepiped. When the Sun passes 100 000 AU, it goes back to 0 AU (to the beginning). The planets, the Moon and the camera (when it follows a celestial body) move back together with the Sun, so that the solar system stays intact.
The way the camera is positioned/moved will be specified in separate requirements.

The ecliptic plane (where the Earth and the Sun always lie) passes through the middle of this parallelepiped. The ecliptic plane has a grid made of squares with sides of 10 AU.
All planets have the eccentricities known/calculated at present and move relative to the Sun on Keplerian orbits.
All planets except the Earth move in the planes known/calculated at present relative to the ecliptic.
Each celestial body displays its rotation axis: a thin light orange line that passes through the poles and goes out of the surface of the body on both sides, up to 2.5 radii from the centre of the body (long enough for its name to fit above the surface, see below), oriented according to the real axial tilt of the body. The body spins around this axis.
The celestial bodies are shown with real surface maps, made from spacecraft and telescope images (for example from NASA). Where a body has never been completely photographed, the missing part is filled in so that it matches the photographed surface around it. The sources of the maps are shown in the program.
Each celestial body also displays three short axes that pass through its centre, parallel to the x, y and z axes of absolute space: red for x, green for y and blue for z. They are thin lines that go out of the surface of the body on both sides, up to 3.75 radii from the centre of the body (1.5 times the length of the rotation axis). The rotation axis and these short axes are displayed only when the body is seen from close (its radius on the screen is at least 30 pixels); from farther away they would be too small to be useful.
The name of each axis is written in the line, in the colour of the line, on both parts outside the body: the line is interrupted and the name is written in the gap ("line  x axis  line"), turned along the line and never upside down. On the x, y and z axes the name shows the sign of that half: "x axis" on the positive half and "-x axis" on the negative half (the same for y and z), so we can see for example which side is above the ecliptic plane (+z) and which is below it (-z). On both halves of the rotation axis the name is "rotation axis".
The names appear and disappear in two groups: the names of the x, y and z axes appear all at once, and the two names of the rotation axis appear together. A group is shown when the body is big enough on the screen for the longest name of the group to fit in its line (with a piece of line on both sides) when the axis is seen from the side; therefore the short names of the x, y and z axes are shown from farther away than the longer "rotation axis" names.
A single name (and its gap) is hidden, and its line is drawn whole, when: there is not enough room for it in the line (the axis is seen at a slant, nearly along its length), that part of the axis is behind the body or behind the camera, or the name would touch the outline of the body on the screen.
All celestial bodies leave a trail of their passage through absolute space covering a period of 200 years; the trail fades out gradually over the following 50 years.
When the Sun passes from 100 000 AU to 0 AU, the trails are interrupted at that point (they are not connected across this jump).

REQ-01.1
The following is permanently displayed in the upper right part of the screen:
  - three buttons arranged horizontally: on the left a button for decreasing the speed, in the centre a start/stop button and on the right a button for increasing the speed. The speed steps are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 months/sec and 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 years/sec.
Above the buttons, the current year (between 0 and 2000) and the current speed are displayed (the speed below the current year and above the buttons).
  - below the speed and start/stop buttons there is the following information about the current reference system (which can be absolute space, the Sun, one of the planets (Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune or Pluto) or the Earth's natural satellite, the Moon):
    - the name
    - a short general description
    - the position of the observation camera in absolute space (x, y, z) in AU, with 2 decimals. When the camera is closer than 0.01 AU to the centre of a celestial body, the position is also displayed in km (with these small distances the values in AU would show 0.00).
    - the distance from the Sun (in AU, with 2 decimals, or in km when it is smaller than 0.01 AU)
    - for planets, the period of revolution around the Sun, in Earth years, with 2 decimals; for the Moon, the period of revolution around the Earth, in Earth days
The dimensions of the celestial bodies (for example the radius), including those of the Sun, are too small to be expressed in AU; they are displayed in km.
When no part of the reference celestial body is visible on the screen (also when it is behind the camera), a small arrow is displayed near the edge of the screen, pointing towards that body, with its name next to it (in the colour of the body). The arrow is placed on the line that goes from the centre of the screen in the direction of the body, so it shows in which direction the view must be turned (for example with the mouse) to bring the body on the screen; if the body is exactly behind the camera, the arrow points down. The arrow disappears as soon as any part of the body (or the dot that represents it from big distances) is on the screen. In "absolute space reference system" mode the arrow points to the Sun. The arrow is drawn over the information panels.

REQ-01.2
This requirement describes the menu.

The menu is positioned in the upper left part of the screen. In the upper left corner there is a ">" sign (expands the menu). When the menu is expanded, this sign moves to the upper right part of the menu and changes to "<".
The menu is a rectangle with its long side on the left side of the upper part of the screen and its short side on the left part of the top edge of the screen.
When the menu is collapsed, a thickened line is visible on the upper left edge of the screen. If the mouse touches this line, the menu expands, and when the mouse leaves the menu area the menu collapses again (it stays expanded only if the ">" button is pressed).

The menu contains the following entries:
 - reference system. When pressed, a submenu opens on the right, listing vertically: absolute space, the planets, the Moon and the Sun. The reference system used in the simulation is selected from there.
 - about. When this entry is pressed, the intro opens.
 - controls. When this entry is pressed, the description of the simulator's controls is displayed.

REQ-01.intro
The program starts with an "intro". This intro is an automatic presentation of the program, with graphics and text; the user can stop it, go to the next/previous page or "skip intro".
The text appears on a parchment scroll unrolled horizontally. Initially the characters of the text do not appear; instead some random characters appear, changing rapidly in their positions (random letters and digits). After about 2 sec the first two lines of text settle to the correct characters over a period of one second, after which the following lines settle, at an interval of 0.5 sec from one another.

REQ-02
This requirement describes the control of the observation camera by keyboard and mouse.

The "yaw" plane of the observation camera will always be parallel to the ecliptic plane. The "roll" angle of the camera is 0 (picture upright) or 180 degrees (picture upside down), regardless of the camera position in absolute space; in other words, the top and bottom edges of the screen will always be parallel to the ecliptic plane. The roll angle can become 180 degrees only through the meridian movement in "celestial body reference system" mode (see REQ-02.2). The mouse sensitivity is 0.081 degrees of "yaw" or "pitch" angle per pixel of mouse movement (the movement is the one reported by the operating system, so the pointer speed settings of the operating system also influence it).
The camera angles are always kept within fixed limits, also internally in the program, no matter how long the camera keeps turning or moving: the "yaw" angle is always between -180 and +180 degrees; the "pitch" angle is always between -90 and +90 degrees, in both camera modes; the "roll" angle is either 0 or 180 degrees. Every other angle used to describe the position or the orientation of the camera or of the celestial bodies (for example the rotation angle of a body around its own axis) is also kept within a limited range (for example -180 to +180 or 0 to 360 degrees), so that no value grows without limit.
The speed of the camera through absolute space depends on its distance from a celestial body as follows: if the centre of a celestial body is closer than 2 AU to the camera, the camera's travel speed (in any direction) will be 0.4 AU/sec; if the distance is between 2 and 3 AU, the speed is 0.6 AU/sec; if the distance is between 3 and 4 AU, the speed will be 0.8 AU/sec; if it is between 4 and 8 AU, the speed will be 1.8 AU/sec; and if it is greater than 8 AU, the speed is 4 AU/sec.

REQ-02.1
Camera control in "absolute space reference system" mode

In this mode the camera moves through absolute space only through the user's commands. In this mode a little cross is displayed in the middle of the screen, in order to be more clear where the camera view points. The cross is made of 4 segments (up, down, left and right) with a little empty space in the middle, so that the exact point the camera looks at remains visible (segments 8 pixels long and 2 pixels thick, empty space of 6 pixels, white with a thin dark outline so that it is visible on light and dark backgrounds).
In this case the camera is controlled as follows:
   - by rolling the mouse wheel forward, or by pressing the "up arrow" key or "w", the camera moves forward along the depth axis ("dolly move"). By rolling the mouse wheel backward, or by pressing the "down arrow" key or "s", the camera moves backward.
   - by moving the mouse forward and backward, the "pitch" angle of the camera changes. The pitch angle can only be changed between -90 and +90 degrees. When the mouse is moved forward, the view direction goes "down" (towards the bottom edge of the screen), and when it is moved backward, the view direction goes "up" (towards the top edge of the screen).
   - by moving the mouse left and right, the "yaw" angle of the camera changes. When the mouse is moved to the right, the view direction moves towards the right edge of the screen; when the mouse is moved to the left, the view direction moves towards the left edge of the screen.
   - by pressing the "d" or "right arrow" keys the camera moves to the right, and by pressing the "a" or "left arrow" keys the camera moves to the left (a "truck" type of movement, also called "strafe" in games). The movement is parallel to the ecliptic plane and perpendicular to the view direction; the view direction ("yaw" and "pitch" angles) does not change.
   - by pressing the "PgUp" or "PgDown" keys, the camera moves away from/closer to the ecliptic plane (a pedestal/crane type of movement is performed), depending on the position of the camera (above or below the ecliptic plane).
With the "PgUp" key the camera moves "up" (towards the top of the screen).
With the "PgDown" key the camera moves "down" (towards the bottom of the screen).

REQ-02.2
Camera control in "celestial body reference system" mode

In this mode the camera moves through absolute space in a combined way, through the user's commands and through the motion of the celestial body.
In this case the camera's reference system depends on the centre of the celestial body chosen by the user (a celestial body that moves through absolute space).

In this case the camera is controlled as follows:
   - by rolling the mouse wheel forward and backward, the camera's reference system moves closer to or farther from the centre of the chosen celestial body. The minimum distance of the camera from the centre of the celestial body is 2*R (where R is the radius of the celestial body). In order to have the celestial bodies visible from big distances, the apparent radius of a body (the angle under which its radius is seen from the camera) does not decrease below about 0.34 degrees (the apparent radius of Pluto seen from 200 000 km). This minimum is the same for all celestial bodies, so from big distances the Sun and all the planets are seen as dots of the same size. This behavior is not real but is very good for simulation purposes.
   - by moving the mouse forward and backward, the "pitch" angle of the camera changes. When the mouse is moved forward, the view direction goes "down" (towards the bottom edge of the screen), and when it is moved backward, the view direction goes "up" (towards the top edge of the screen). The pitch angle can only be changed between -90 and +90 degrees, as in "absolute space reference system" mode. When the picture is upside down (roll 180 degrees), the mouse still moves the view as seen on the screen: forward towards the bottom edge of the screen, right towards the right edge.
   - by moving the mouse left and right, the "yaw" angle of the camera changes. When the mouse is moved to the right, the view direction moves towards the right edge of the screen; when it is moved to the left, the view direction moves towards the left edge of the screen.
   - by pressing the "up arrow" or "w" keys, the observation camera moves "forward" (in the direction we are looking, taken on the screen as forward/up) by turning around the horizontal axis that passes through the centre of the reference celestial body and is parallel to the top and bottom edges of the screen (the "pitch" axis of the camera: it is parallel to the ecliptic plane and perpendicular to the horizontal projection of the view direction). By pressing the "down arrow" or "s" keys, the movement is the same but "backward".
In this case the whole camera turns rigidly around this axis, together with its view direction (a "turntable" movement, like the turntable orbit of CAD programs): normally only the "pitch" angle changes, the "yaw" and "roll" angles do not change. The distance from the camera to the centre of the celestial body and the angle between the segment joining the centre of the celestial body with the camera and the view direction of the camera remain exactly constant, so the celestial body and everything around it stay still on the screen while the camera moves. When the view direction passes straight up or straight down (pitch +90 or -90 degrees), the camera continues the same movement and the picture does not jump; to keep the "pitch" angle between -90 and +90 degrees, at that moment the "yaw" angle changes by 180 degrees and the "roll" angle changes from 0 to 180 degrees (the picture is now upside down) or from 180 back to 0 degrees. When the key is released nothing changes on the screen: if the picture is upside down it stays so (roll 180 degrees) until a meridian movement brings it back over the vertical, the "Home" key is pressed, or the camera is switched to "absolute space reference system" mode (that mode is always upright: the view direction stays the same and the picture turns the right way up).
The camera moves on a circle in a plane perpendicular to the ecliptic. When the view direction lies in the vertical plane that contains the camera and the centre of the celestial body (for example when the camera looks at the centre of the body), this circle is centred on the centre of the celestial body and passes over the axis that is perpendicular to the ecliptic and passes through the centre of the body; otherwise it is a smaller circle, centred on the turning axis.
This movement is equivalent to moving the camera along a meridian of that celestial body, if its rotation axis were perpendicular to the ecliptic and the camera looked at its centre.
   - by pressing the "right arrow" or "d" keys, the centre of the observation camera moves to the right on a circle centred on the axis that is perpendicular to the ecliptic plane and passes through the centre of the reference celestial body, in a plane parallel to the ecliptic plane. By pressing the "left arrow" or "a" keys, we have the same movement but in the opposite direction.
In this case the "yaw" angle of the camera in absolute space will be modified automatically, so that the angle between the segment joining the centre of the celestial body with the camera and the view direction of the camera remains constant.
This movement is equivalent to moving the camera along a parallel of that celestial body, if its rotation axis were perpendicular to the ecliptic.
   - by pressing the "Home" key, the camera returns to a known (predefined) position and view direction. The details are to be clarified later.

IMPL-01
Implementation note: precision of large coordinates ("floating origin").

The absolute space is 100 000 AU long. Graphics cards compute with 32-bit numbers, which at 100 000 AU have a rounding error of about 900 000 km (more than twice the distance between the Earth and the Moon). Therefore everything drawn on the screen (celestial bodies, trails, orbits, grid, axes and other long lines) is computed relative to the camera or to a reference point near it, not in absolute coordinates. Only far away objects keep large coordinates; for them the error is much smaller than one pixel.
   - long straight lines (axes, grid lines, edges of the parallelepiped) are rebuilt around the camera every frame: short pieces near the camera and longer pieces (doubling in length) farther away, as in the one body simulation.
   - trails and orbits are stored relative to a nearby reference point (for example the current position of the Sun), not in absolute coordinates.
   - the grid of 10 AU squares (about 10 000 lines over the length of the parallelepiped) is not drawn line by line: either only the lines near the camera are drawn, or the grid is computed for each pixel by the graphics card.
