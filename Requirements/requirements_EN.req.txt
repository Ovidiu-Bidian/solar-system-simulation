REQ-01
This requirement describes the general functionality of this simulation.

	The purpose of the simulation is to present the motion of some celestial bodies of our solar system (the Sun, Mercury, Venus, the Earth, the Earth's satellite the Moon, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune and Pluto) through absolute space (and their trails, see REQ-03 for details) and relative to each other.
	The Sun moves through space (relative to the nearby stars) with about 20 km/s and through absolute space with about 230 km/s (according to current information available); also according to current data it describes a circle with an enormous radius around the center of the galaxy. Because of that in this simulation the path of the Sun is a straight line.

	The absolute space used in this simulation is a parallelepiped with a length of 1 000 AU (so that the trail of Pluto can show almost one complete loop of its spiral, see REQ-03), a width of 100 AU (slightly larger than Pluto's orbit) and a height of 40 AU (slightly larger than Pluto's orbit highest/lowest points). It is longer towards -x, where the trails of the bodies go (see REQ-03).
	The coordinate system is the following:
   - the origin (point 0) is the center of the Sun. It is not the center of the parallelepiped: the parallelepiped goes from x = -950 AU to x = +50 AU, from y = -50 AU to y = +50 AU and from z = -20 AU to z = +20 AU.
   - the x0y plane is the real ecliptic plane (the plane of Earth's orbit, J2000): a rectangle 1 000 AU long and 100 AU wide, at half of the height of the parallelepiped (20 AU from the top and from the bottom). The x axis goes from -950 AU to +50 AU, the y axis from -50 AU to +50 AU.
   - the z axis is perpendicular to the x0y plane in point 0. It goes 20 AU "up" (+20 AU) and 20 AU "down" (-20 AU).

	The camera that observes the motion of the celestial bodies can be moved only inside this parallelepiped. When the camera reaches a wall of the parallelepiped it stops there: any movement that would take it outside is blocked (movements along the wall or away from it remain possible). The way the camera is positioned/moved is specified in separate requirements (see REQ-02).
	The ecliptic plane passes through the middle of this parallelepiped. The ecliptic plane has a grid made of squares with sides of 4 AU (the grid lines are at multiples of 4 AU).
	The Sun stays in point 0 and does not move. The bodies are lit by the Sun: the light comes from the center of the Sun, with the same strength at every distance.
	All planets move relative to the Sun on their real Keplerian orbits, and the Moon around the Earth (see REQ-03). When an orbit appears in the view of the camera, its name is written in the line of the orbit, in the same way as the names of the axes of a celestial body (for example "--- Earth's orbit ---"): the line is interrupted and the name is written in the gap, along the line and never upside down. The name is placed on the visible part of the orbit closest to the center of the screen. If the orbit is too small on the screen (smaller than about 3 times the length of its name), the name is not displayed. When the celestial body comes close to the name of its orbit (on the screen, closer than the length of the name), the name disappears; the body goes on normally along its orbit and the name appears again after the body has passed.

	Each celestial body displays its rotation axis: a thin light orange line that passes through the poles and goes out of the surface of the body on both sides, up to 2.5 radii from the center of the body (long enough for its name to fit above the surface, see below), oriented in its real direction in space (north pole direction from IAU). The body spins around this axis (see REQ-03).
	The celestial bodies are shown with real surface maps, made from spacecraft and telescope images (for example from NASA). Where a body has never been completely photographed, the missing part is filled in so that it matches the photographed surface around it (for example the southern part of Pluto, south of about -30 degrees latitude, is filled in with the average color of the nearest photographed surface, blended smoothly into the photo, with a fine painted pattern). The sources of the maps are shown in the program.
	In "celestial body reference system" mode the reference celestial body also displays three short axes that pass through its center, parallel to the x, y and z axes of the coordinate system: red for x, green for y and blue for z. They are thin lines that go out of the surface of the body on both sides, up to 5 radii from the center of the body (2 times the length of the rotation axis). These axes are not displayed on the other celestial bodies, nor in "absolute space reference system" mode.
	The x, y and z axes of the coordinate system are not drawn over their whole length, only these short pieces through the center of the reference celestial body.
The rotation axis and these short axes are displayed at any distance; only their names depend on the size of the body on the screen (see below).
	The name of each axis is written in the line, in the color of the line, on both parts outside the body: the line is interrupted and the name is written in the gap ("--- x axis ---"), turned along the line and never upside down.
The name is not in the middle of its part but towards the end of the axis: of the line left visible on that part, about 80% is between the body and the name and about 20% between the name and the end of the axis (measured on the screen).
On the x, y and z axes the name shows the sign of that half: "+x axis" on the positive half and "-x axis" on the negative half (the same for y and z), so we can see for example which side is above the ecliptic plane (+z) and which is below it (-z). On both halves of the rotation axis the name is "rotation axis".
	The names appear and disappear in two groups: the names of the x, y and z axes appear all at once, and the two names of the rotation axis appear together. A group name is shown when the body is big enough on the screen for the longest name of the group to fit in its line (with a piece of line on both sides) when the axis is seen from the side; therefore the short names of the x, y and z axes are shown/seen from farther away than the longer "rotation axis" names.
	A single name (and its gap) is hidden, and its line is drawn whole, when:
 - there is not enough room for it in the line (the axis is seen at a slant, nearly along its length)
 - that part of the axis is behind the body or behind the camera
 - the name would touch the outline of the body on the screen.

REQ-01.1
	General rule for the screen: during the simulation only the essential information is displayed, so that most of the screen is free for seeing the bodies, their orbits and trails. Everything else is reached from the menu (see REQ-01.2).

	The following information about the current reference system (which can be absolute space, the Sun, one of the planets (Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune or Pluto) or the Earth's natural satellite, the Moon) is displayed permanently in the upper right part of the screen:
   - the current camera mode ("absolute space" or "celestial body") and the name of the reference celestial body
   - the speed of the body in absolute space (in km/s) (extremely important, this is one of the main topics of this simulation): the size of the sum of its two velocities, its velocity on its orbit (for the Moon, its velocity around the Earth together with the velocity of the Earth around the Sun) and the velocity of the solar system through absolute space (the velocity of the Sun, along +x). So it is between v_abs - v_orbit and v_abs + v_orbit (v_abs: the speed of the Sun in absolute space, v_orbit: the speed of the body on its orbit); for the Sun it is v_abs. In "absolute space reference system" mode it is the speed of the last chosen reference celestial body.
   - the position of the observation camera in absolute space (x, y, z) in AU, with 2 decimals. When the camera is closer than 0.01 AU to the center of the reference celestial body, its position relative to the center of that body is also displayed in km ("From <body> x, y, z"; with these small distances the values in AU would hardly change).
   - the "pan" and "tilt" angles of the camera, in degrees
   - the distance from the camera to the center of the reference celestial body (in AU with 2 decimals, or in km when it is smaller than 0.01 AU)
   - the distance from the camera to the center of the Sun (in AU with 2 decimals, or in km when it is smaller than 0.01 AU)
   - the travel speed of the camera (see REQ-02)

	Below the space where is the above information is a button like "V" which gives the user possibility to have more information about the body. that button should change to "^" when additional information is displayed. The additional information is this:
   - a short general description of the reference celestial body ("ice blue giant", etc)
   - the speed on its orbit (in km/s); for the Sun, its speed in absolute space (about 230 km/s)
   - the orbit tilt (to the ecliptic); for the Sun "-" (it does not apply)
   - for planets, the period of revolution around the Sun, in Earth years, with 2 decimals; for the Moon, the period of revolution around the Earth, in Earth days; for the Sun "-" (its orbit around the center of the galaxy takes about 230 million years, too long to be useful). The period of rotation around its own axis (in Earth days) is also displayed.
   - the dimensions of the celestial body: the radius in km (the dimensions of the celestial bodies, including those of the Sun, are too small to be expressed in AU), the volume in km3 and the mass in kg

	When no part of the reference celestial body is visible on the screen (also when it is behind the camera), a small arrow is displayed near the edge of the screen, pointing towards that body, with its name next to it (in the color of the body). The arrow is placed on the line that goes from the center of the screen in the direction of the body, so it shows in which direction the view must be turned (for example with the mouse) to bring the body on the screen; if the body is exactly behind the camera, the arrow points down. The arrow disappears as soon as any part of the body (or the dot that represents it from big distances) is on the screen.
The arrow is displayed in both camera modes (in "absolute space reference system" mode it points to the last chosen reference celestial body) and is drawn over the information panels.

	In the lower right corner of the screen there is a small panel with three buttons in a row: "-", "Run/Stop" and "+"; underneath them the current simulation speed is displayed. The current date or year of the simulation is not displayed: it has no real meaning, only the motion shows the flow of time. In the program the time is counted in seconds, in a limited interval (for example from 0 to 100 seconds, then again from 0), so that it never grows without limit; when the count passes from 100 back to 0 nothing can be seen on the screen: all the celestial bodies and trails stay the same.
The "Run/Stop" button stops and starts again the motion of all bodies (orbits, spins and trails). While the motion is stopped the camera can still be moved in both modes.
The "-" and "+" buttons choose the simulation speed (see REQ-03) among: 1, 2, 3 weeks/sec, 1, 3, 4, 6 months/sec and 1 year/sec, displayed as "1 wk/s", "2 wk/s", "3 wk/s", "1 mo/s", "3 mo/s", "4 mo/s", "6 mo/s" and "1 yr/s". "-" chooses the next slower speed and "+" the next faster one; at the slowest speed "-" is disabled (grayed out), and at the fastest speed "+" is disabled. The program starts with 3 months/sec.
The speed chosen is kept when the motion is stopped and started again.

REQ-01.2
This requirement describes the menu.

	In the upper left corner of the screen there is a "Menu" button. It opens a list of menu items, which closes again when an item is chosen or when the user clicks anywhere else.
The menu contains the following items:
   - "Reference system": when chosen, a submenu opens on the right, listing vertically: absolute space, the Sun, the planets and the Moon. The reference system used in the simulation is selected from there. By pressing "m" key" the reference system is switched between "absolute space reference mode" and "body reference mode" (selects the last selected body when moving from absolute to body reference).
   - "Help". Has 3 submenus:
	- "Intro" (see REQ-01.3).
	- "Controls": shows the list of the controls (keys and mouse) of the current camera mode, in a panel in the lower left corner of the screen; the list changes when the mode is switched. The panel is not displayed when the program starts; it is closed with the "x" in its upper right corner or by choosing "Controls" in the menu again. While the panel is displayed, the "Controls" item is marked with a check mark.
	- "About". Information about version, etc (was made with Claude code, etc).

REQ-01.3
This requirement describes the Intro of the program

	The program starts with an "intro". This intro is an automatic presentation of the program, with graphics and text; the user can stop it, go to the next/previous page or "skip intro".
The text appears on a parchment scroll unrolled horizontally. Initially the characters of the text do not appear; instead some random characters appear, changing rapidly in their positions (random letters and digits). After about 2 sec the first two lines of text settle to the correct characters over a period of one second, after which the following lines settle, at an interval of 0.5 sec from one another.

REQ-02
This requirement describes the control of the observation camera by keyboard and mouse.

	The observation camera works like a surveillance camera mounted on a pan/tilt head. Its orientation (the direction it looks in and how the picture is turned) is described only by two angles, "pan" and "tilt"; the camera never turns around its own view direction (except during the 2 second turn when switching to "absolute space reference system" mode, see below).

	Each angle is defined by the axis (line) the camera turns around:
   - pan: the camera turns around the pan axis, a line through the camera that is always perpendicular to the ecliptic plane (parallel to the z axis), whatever the tilt. It does not turn with the camera when it tilts, so it is not always the line through the top and bottom sides of the camera: for example at tilt +90 or -90 degrees the pan axis is the same as the view direction. Pan 0 means the camera head faces the +x direction; positive pan angles turn it towards +y (counterclockwise when looking from above). The pan angle is between -180 and +180 degrees; turning further past +180 degrees continues from -180 degrees, and the other way round.
   - tilt: the camera turns around the tilt axis, a line through the left and right sides of the camera, parallel to the ecliptic plane and to the top and bottom edges of the screen (perpendicular to the direction the camera head faces). Tilt 0 means the view direction is parallel to the ecliptic plane, in the direction the head faces, with the picture upright (top edge of the screen to +z axis). Positive tilt angles raise the view direction (towards +z), negative tilt angles lower it (towards -z); +90 degrees is straight up and -90 degrees is straight down. Like a pan/tilt head with full tilt range, the tilt can go past the vertical: between +90 and +180 degrees the camera has tilted over the top, and between -90 and -180 degrees it has tilted under the bottom; in both cases it looks backward (away from the direction the head faces) and the picture is upside down. +180 and -180 degrees are the same orientation (looking horizontally backward, picture upside down); turning further past it continues from the other end.

	Therefore the top and bottom edges of the screen are always parallel to the ecliptic plane, regardless of the camera position in absolute space: the picture is upright when the tilt is between -90 and +90 degrees and upside down otherwise.
When the picture is upright, the user can move the tilt with the mouse only in the range -90 to +90 degrees. This is mandatory because a tilted ecliptic on the screen, and worse than that, a tilted ecliptic while upside down, makes the user completely lose orientation in space.
	The tilt can be carried over +90 or -90 degrees only by the meridian movement in "celestial body reference system" mode (see REQ-02.2), never by the user with the mouse. In "absolute space reference system" mode the picture is always upright (tilt between -90 and +90 degrees).
Examples: pan 0, tilt 0: looking horizontally along +x. Pan 90, tilt 0: looking horizontally along +y. Pan 0, tilt -60: looking towards +x and 60 degrees down. Pan 0, tilt +120: the camera has tilted over the top; it looks towards -x and 60 degrees up, with the picture upside down (this can happen only in "celestial body reference system" meridian movement).
	The camera angles are always kept within fixed limits, also internally in the program, no matter how long the camera keeps turning or moving: the "pan" and "tilt" angles are always between -180 and +180 degrees. Every other angle used to describe the position or the orientation of the camera or of the celestial bodies (for example the rotation angle of a body around its own axis) is also kept within a limited range (for example -180 to +180 or 0 to 360 degrees), so that no value grows without limit.

	After the intro, the program starts in "celestial body reference system" mode, with the camera in the Home position (see REQ-02.2) of the starting reference celestial body, Earth.
	The "m" key switches between the "absolute space reference system" mode and the "celestial body reference system" mode (with the last chosen reference celestial body). When the mode is switched, the camera keeps its position and its view direction. When switching from "celestial body reference system" mode to "absolute space reference system" mode while the picture is upside down, the camera keeps its position and its view direction and the picture turns upright smoothly, around the center of the screen, in 2 seconds (not instantly, because a sudden turn confuses the user). The tilt is brought back between -90 and +90 degrees and the pan changes by 180 degrees, so that the view direction stays the same (for example pan 0, tilt +120 becomes pan 180, tilt +60).

	The mouse controls the camera only while the left mouse button is held down ("drag"). While the button is held down the mouse cursor is not visible on the screen; it appears again when the button is released.
	The mouse sensitivity is 0.05 degrees of "pan" or "tilt" angle per pixel of mouse movement (the movement is the one reported by the operating system, so the pointer speed settings of the operating system also influence it).
	Moving the mouse "forward" means moving it away from the user (the cursor would go towards the top of the screen), moving it "backward" means moving it towards the user.

	The speed of the camera through absolute space depends on its distance from the center of the nearest celestial body (the Sun, a planet or the Moon). The camera's travel speed (in any direction) is:
   - distance from 5 million km to 0.5 AU: 0.4 AU/sec
   - distance from 0.5 AU to 1 AU: 1 AU/sec
   - distance from 1 AU to 2 AU: 2 AU/sec
   - distance from 2 AU to 4 AU: 4 AU/sec
   - distance from 4 AU to 8 AU: 8 AU/sec
   - distance from 8 AU to 12 AU: 10 AU/sec
   - distance greater than 12 AU: 12 AU/sec (the maximum speed of the camera)
   - distance smaller than 5 million km: the speed is half of the current distance per second (for example 2 500 000 km/sec at 5 million km, 2 377 km/sec at 4 753 km). In this way the camera slows down when approaching the body and never jumps over it. With the minimum apparent size of the bodies (see REQ-03), this limit is noticed mainly near the Sun: the sudden drop of the speed shows that the camera is close to it.
The camera never moves, in one picture on the screen, more than half of the distance to the center of the nearest celestial body, so that also a slow picture (a computer that is busy for a moment) cannot make the camera jump over a body.
	In "celestial body reference system" mode, the movements on meridians and parallels (see REQ-02.2) are done with a constant angular speed of 30 degrees/sec, regardless of the distance to the body.

REQ-02.1
Camera control in "absolute space reference system" mode

	In this mode the camera moves through absolute space only through the user's commands; it does not depend on the movement of the celestial bodies, except for the push described at the end of this requirement. In this mode a little cross is displayed in the middle of the screen, in order to be more clear where the camera view points. The cross is made of 4 segments (up, down, left and right) with a little empty space in the middle, so that the exact point the camera looks at remains visible (segments 8 pixels long and 2 pixels thick, empty space of 6 pixels, white with a thin dark outline so that it is visible on light and dark backgrounds).

	In this case the camera is controlled as follows:
   - by rolling the mouse wheel forward, or by pressing the "up arrow" key or "w", the camera moves forward along the depth axis ("dolly move"). By rolling the mouse wheel backward, or by pressing the "down arrow" key or "s", the camera moves backward.
   - by moving the mouse forward and backward, the "tilt" angle of the camera changes. In this mode the tilt angle can only be changed between -90 and +90 degrees (the picture is always upright). If the tilt angle is -90 degrees and the user still moves the mouse forward nothing happens. Similarly if the tilt angle is +90 degrees and the user moves the mouse backward nothing happens.
When the mouse is moved forward, the view direction goes "down" (towards the bottom edge of the screen), and when it is moved backward, the view direction goes "up" (towards the top edge of the screen).
   - by moving the mouse left and right, the "pan" angle of the camera changes. When the mouse is moved to the right, the view direction moves towards the left edge of the screen; when it is moved to the left, the view direction moves towards the right edge of the screen. Together with the forward and backward movement, the image follows the mouse, as if the user grabbed the image and pulled it ("grab the image").
   - by pressing the "d" or "right arrow" keys the camera moves to the right, and by pressing the "a" or "left arrow" keys the camera moves to the left (a "truck" type of movement, also called "strafe" in games). The movement is parallel to the ecliptic plane and perpendicular to the view direction; the view direction ("pan" and "tilt" angles) does not change.
   - by pressing the "PgUp" or "PgDown" keys, the camera moves away from/closer to the ecliptic plane (a pedestal/crane type of movement is performed), depending on the position of the camera (above or below the ecliptic plane). With the "PgUp" key the camera moves "up" (towards the top of the screen). With the "PgDown" key the camera moves "down" (towards the bottom of the screen).

	The camera never comes closer than 2*R to the center of a celestial body (R is the radius of that body; the same minimum as in "celestial body reference system" mode). The planets and the Moon move, so a body can come to a camera that stands still (for example a camera placed on the orbit of a planet); in this case the body pushes the camera away:
   - when the body passes the camera closer than 2*R, the camera is pushed sideways, perpendicular to the path of the body, so that it ends 2*R away from that path, on the side where it was. The body passes by and the camera stays next to the orbit. This is where the camera would get if it slid around the side of the body; on the time scale of the simulation a body moves much more than 2*R between two pictures on the screen (Pluto about 500 000 km, Earth about 3 million km at 3 months/sec), so the passing is checked along the whole path of the body between two pictures, not only at its new position.
   - when the camera is exactly on the path of the body, "sideways" is not defined: the camera is pushed parallel to the ecliptic plane, in the direction opposite to the Sun (seen from the camera's position).
   - when the body stops closer than 2*R before reaching the camera (it has not passed it yet), the camera is pushed straight away from the center of the body, to 2*R; if the camera is (almost) straight in front of the body on its orbit, it is pushed instead parallel to the ecliptic plane, in the direction opposite to the Sun, so that it is not carried along in front of the body.
The Sun does not move in the coordinate system from the simulation, so it never pushes the camera; it only stops the camera at 2*R from its center.

REQ-02.2
Camera control in "celestial body reference system" mode

	In this mode the camera moves around the chosen celestial body through the user's commands.
	In this case the camera's reference system is the center of the celestial body chosen by the user (see REQ-01.2). The camera travels together with the body along its orbit: its position relative to the center of the body and its view direction stay the same, so the body stays still on the screen while the stars, the grid and the other bodies move. If the camera reaches a wall of the parallelepiped it stops there, and the body moves away from it. The other bodies push the camera away as in "absolute space reference system" mode (see REQ-02.1).

	In this case the camera is controlled as follows:
   - by rolling the mouse wheel forward and backward, the camera's reference system moves closer to or farther from the center of the chosen celestial body. The minimum distance of the camera from the center of the celestial body is 2*R (where R is the radius of the celestial body). In order to have the celestial bodies visible from big distances, the apparent radius of a body (the angle under which its radius is seen from the camera) does not decrease below about 0.34 degrees (the apparent radius of Pluto seen from 200 000 km). This minimum is the same for all the planets and the Moon, so from big distances they are seen as dots of the same size. The minimum apparent radius of the Sun is 2 times bigger (maybe 3 times, to be decided after seeing it on the screen), so that from big distances the Sun is always the biggest dot ("who's the boss").
This behavior is not real but for simulation purposes is very good.
   - by moving the mouse forward and backward, the "tilt" angle of the camera changes the same way as in "absolute space reference system" mode. When the mouse is moved forward, the view direction goes "down" (towards the bottom edge of the screen), and when it is moved backward, the view direction goes "up" (towards the top edge of the screen).
The mouse can never carry the tilt over +90 or -90 degrees: when the picture is upright the tilt stays between -90 and +90 degrees, as in "absolute space reference system" mode.
The picture can be upside down only after a meridian movement over the vertical (made only from the keyboard, see below); then the tilt stays between +90 and +180 degrees or between -180 and -90 degrees (passing through +/-180 degrees, a horizontal view looking backward, is allowed). If the user keeps moving the mouse towards +90 or -90 degrees nothing happens. So after a meridian movement the mouse keeps the picture on its side: upright stays upright and upside down stays upside down; only a meridian movement, the "Home" key or the switch to "absolute space reference system" mode can change it. When the picture is upside down, the mouse still moves the view as seen on the screen: forward towards the bottom edge of the screen, right towards the left edge.
   - by moving the mouse left and right, the "pan" angle of the camera changes. When the mouse is moved to the right, the view direction moves towards the left edge of the screen; when it is moved to the left, the view direction moves towards the right edge of the screen. Together with the forward and backward movement, the image follows the mouse, as if the user grabbed the image and pulled it ("grab the image").

   - by pressing the "up arrow" or "w" keys, the observation camera moves "forward" (in the direction we are looking, taken on the screen as forward/up) by turning around the horizontal axis that passes through the center of the reference celestial body and is parallel to the top and bottom edges of the screen (parallel to the tilt axis of the camera: it is parallel to the ecliptic plane and perpendicular to the horizontal projection of the view direction). By pressing the "down arrow" or "s" keys, the movement is the same but "backward".
	This is called in this simulation "meridian movement".

	In this case the whole camera turns rigidly around this axis, together with its view direction (a "turntable" movement, like the turntable orbit of CAD programs): only the "tilt" angle changes, the "pan" angle does not change. The distance from the camera to the center of the celestial body and the angle between the segment joining the center of the celestial body with the camera and the view direction of the camera remain exactly constant, so the celestial body and everything around it stay still on the screen while the camera moves.
	When the view direction passes straight up or straight down (tilt +90 or -90 degrees), the camera continues the same movement and the picture does not jump: the tilt simply goes past +90 (or -90) degrees and the picture is now upside down; when it goes back over the vertical the picture is upright again. The pan angle does not change. When the key is released nothing changes on the screen: if the picture is upside down it stays so until a meridian movement brings it back over the vertical, the "Home" key is pressed, or the camera is switched to "absolute space reference system" mode (that mode is always upright: the view direction stays the same and the picture turns the right way up, see REQ-02).
	The camera moves on a circle in a plane perpendicular to the ecliptic. When the view direction lies in the vertical plane that contains the camera and the center of the celestial body (for example when the camera looks at the center of the body), this circle is centered on the center of the celestial body and passes over the axis that is perpendicular to the ecliptic and passes through the center of the body; otherwise it is a smaller circle, centered on the turning axis.
	This movement is equivalent to moving the camera along a meridian of that celestial body, if its rotation axis were perpendicular to the ecliptic and the camera looked at its center. This is not so in reality, but still in the simulation we should move the camera as described here, and the rotation axis of the body should be the real one. We will see the axis of the celestial body tilted, but still we can move over any point of the body.

   - by pressing the "right arrow" or "d" keys, the center of the observation camera moves to the right on a circle centered on the axis that is perpendicular to the ecliptic plane and passes through the center of the reference celestial body, in a plane parallel to the ecliptic plane. By pressing the "left arrow" or "a" keys, we have the same movement but in the opposite direction.
	This is called in this simulation "parallel movement".

	In this case the "pan" angle of the camera will be modified automatically, so that the angle between the segment joining the center of the celestial body with the camera and the view direction of the camera remains constant.
This movement is equivalent to moving the camera along a parallel of that celestial body, if its rotation axis were perpendicular to the ecliptic. This is not so in reality, but still in the simulation we should move the camera as described here. In this case we won't walk on the real parallels of the celestial body (if the rotation axis of that body is not perpendicular to the ecliptic) but this is not a problem for simulation purposes.

   - by pressing the "Home" key, the camera returns to a known (predefined) position and view direction, relative to the body and to the "body ecliptic" (the plane parallel to the ecliptic that contains the center of the body; its x and y axes are parallel to the x and y axes of the ecliptic, in the same direction).
	That position is: the center of the body is in the center of the screen, the distance to the center of the body is eight times its radius (8*R), 60 degrees above the body ecliptic, "tilt" angle -60 degrees (picture upright), "pan" angle -135 degrees. So the camera is at about +2.83*R in x, +2.83*R in y and +6.93*R in z from the center of the body.
	In this case we are looking exactly at the center of the body, somewhere from an "above" position (towards the ecliptic north pole). The Home position does not depend on the position of the Sun, so the body can be seen on its lit side, on its dark side or in between.

REQ-03
Bodies, orbits, time scale and trails

	The simulation shows the Sun, the planets Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune and Pluto, and the Earth's satellite, the Moon.
   - the Sun: real radius (695 700 km), in point 0, it does not move. It is drawn as a bright self-lit sphere with a soft glow.
   - the planets: real radius, real rotation axis (north pole direction from IAU) and real surface map. They move on their real orbits around the Sun (JPL approximate Keplerian elements, J2000 ecliptic: semi-major axis, eccentricity, inclination, longitude of the ascending node, longitude of perihelion). The motion follows Kepler's laws (faster near the Sun), each planet independently (the planets do not attract each other).
   - the Moon: real radius, real surface map; it moves on its real orbit around the Earth (it travels together with the Earth around the Sun).
   - the planets have the same minimum apparent radius, Moon has 1/2 apparent minimum size compared to those of planets and the Sun a 2 times (maybe 3 times) bigger one (see REQ-02.2), so from big distances the planets are seen as dots of the same size, Moon half the size and the Sun as a bigger dot.
   - the orbit of every planet (and of the Moon around the Earth) is drawn as a thin line in the color of the body (for example Pluto light orange, Earth blue, Mars red, Jupiter tan; the colors of the other bodies are to be decided). Close to a body the line passes exactly through its center.
   - at the start every planet is in the ecliptic plane, at the ascending node of its orbit (where it crosses the ecliptic going up). These are not the real positions of the planets on a given date but this doesn't matter for the scope of simulation.

	Time scale: the time of the simulation is measured in Earth weeks, months and years. The simulation speed (chosen with the "-" and "+" buttons, see REQ-01.1) says how much Earth time passes in one real second: 1, 2, 3 weeks, 1, 3, 4, 6 months or 1 year. The orbits and the trails use this time scale; the real orbital periods are: Mercury about 0.24 years, Venus about 0.62 years, Earth 1 year, Mars about 1.88 years, Jupiter about 11.9 years, Saturn about 29.5 years, Uranus about 84 years, Neptune about 165 years, Pluto about 248 years; the Moon goes around the Earth in about 27.3 days. So one orbit and the trails take:
   - 1 week/sec: the Moon about 3.9 seconds, Mercury about 12.6 seconds, Venus about 32 seconds, Earth about 52 seconds, Jupiter about 10.3 minutes, Pluto about 3.6 hours; the trails move with about 0.08 AU/sec and reach the -x wall in about 3.3 hours.
   - 2 weeks/sec: the Moon about 2 seconds, Mercury about 6.3 seconds, Venus about 16 seconds, Earth about 26 seconds, Jupiter about 5.2 minutes, Pluto about 1.8 hours; the trails move with about 0.16 AU/sec and reach the -x wall in about 1.6 hours.
   - 3 weeks/sec: the Moon about 1.3 seconds, Mercury about 4.2 seconds, Venus about 10.7 seconds, Earth about 17 seconds, Jupiter about 3.4 minutes, Pluto about 1.2 hours; the trails move with about 0.24 AU/sec and reach the -x wall in about 1.1 hours.
   - 1 month/sec: Earth 12 seconds, Jupiter about 2.4 minutes, Pluto about 50 minutes; the trails move with about 0.35 AU/sec and reach the -x wall in about 45 minutes.
   - 3 months/sec: Earth 4 seconds, Jupiter about 48 seconds, Pluto about 16.5 minutes; the trails move with about 1.05 AU/sec and reach the -x wall in about 15 minutes.
   - 4 months/sec: Earth 3 seconds, Jupiter about 36 seconds, Pluto about 12.4 minutes; the trails move with about 1.4 AU/sec and reach the -x wall in about 11 minutes.
   - 6 months/sec: Earth 2 seconds, Jupiter about 24 seconds, Pluto about 8.3 minutes; the trails move with about 2.1 AU/sec and reach the -x wall in about 7.5 minutes.
   - 1 year/sec: Earth 1 second, Jupiter about 12 seconds, Pluto about 4.1 minutes; the trails move with about 4.2 AU/sec and reach the -x wall in about 3.8 minutes.
The shape of the trails does not depend on the simulation speed; only how fast they are formed changes.
	The spin of the bodies is not on this time scale: the real rotation speeds cannot be followed by the human eye anyway, the spin only needs to show that the body turns around its rotation axis. So every body turns once in 10 seconds, at every simulation speed, in its real direction around its real rotation axis. Exception: the Moon always shows the same face to the Earth, so it turns once around its axis in every orbit around the Earth (about 27.3 days), on the time scale of the orbits.

	Trails: the bodies stay in this fixed space, but every body leaves behind a trail: a yellow line, 2 pixels wide. Every point of a trail is created where the body is at that moment (x, y, z) and then moves towards -x, with the speed of the Sun through space, so that its x coordinate decreases in time while y and z stay the same. In this way the trails show the paths of the bodies as if the whole solar system moved towards +x through space: the Sun leaves a straight line and every planet a spiral (helix) around it.
	The speed of the Sun through space is the speed relative to the nearby stars, about 20 km/s (the speed around the center of the galaxy, about 230 km/s, was also tried). The speed that will be used (probably about 230 km/s, see REQ-01) is still to be decided. It uses the same time scale as the orbits: the Sun travels about 4.2 AU in one Earth year (about 1 050 AU in one Pluto orbit); the speed of the trails on the screen depends on the simulation speed (see above). One loop of a spiral is then about 4.2 AU long for Earth, 7.9 AU for Mars, 50 AU for Jupiter and 1 050 AU for Pluto.
	A trail ends at the -x wall of the parallelepiped (x = -950 AU); a point needs about 225 Earth years to get there from the Sun (for the real time, see the time scale above). So the trail of Pluto shows about 90% of one loop of its spiral (at 20 km/s; at a higher speed of the Sun the trails show a smaller part of a loop). The trails are smooth also for the fast planets: a planet leaves a point every 2 degrees of its orbit, also between two pictures on the screen.
	Start of the trail: at every distance the trail of a body starts 2 radii from its center (1 radius above its surface), so that the body looks as if it moved away from its trail at a huge speed. Far away, where a body is drawn larger than real (see the minimum apparent radius above), this distance grows with it, so that it can always be seen.
	Plume: when the camera is closer than 80 radii to the center of a body (any body, the Sun included), the first part of its trail looks like the trail of a plane's engine, showing the energy and the speed of the body: yellow "smoke", 0.3 radii thick and 80 radii long, made of soft puffs that flow away from the body very fast (37.5 radii per second, like high-pressure steam leaving an engine; only while the motion is running). This is only a visual effect: at these distances the real trail is a straight line, made in a fraction of a second. The plume appears gradually: it starts to be seen at 80 radii and is complete at 40 radii, while the line it replaces fades out at the same time. From a quarter of its length the smoke gets thinner, down to the width of the line, and in its last part it fades out while the line under it fades in, so that at the end of the plume only the line remains, exactly as bright as the rest of the trail: there is no visible point where the plume ends and the line begins.

REQ-04
Stars and constellations

	Real constellations are visible from the simulation space: Ursa Major, Ursa Minor, Cassiopeia, Cygnus, Lyra, Pegasus, Andromeda, Auriga, Taurus, Crux, Carina, Centaurus, Octans, Hercules and Columba (the list may be extended later).
Some of them are related to the motion of the Sun through space, which was determined from the light of the stars (their positions over the years and the Doppler shift in their spectra): Hercules contains the solar apex, the point of the sky towards which the Sun moves relative to the nearby stars (not far from Vega, in Lyra); Columba contains the solar antapex, the point the Sun moves away from; Cygnus is in the direction of the Sun's motion around the center of the galaxy. The speed and the direction of the Sun are to be discussed later (see REQ-03).
Their stars are drawn in their real directions on the sky (real right ascension and declination, converted to the ecliptic coordinates of the simulation). The stars are so far away that their directions are the same from every point of the parallelepiped: they do not move when the camera moves through space, only when the view direction changes. They are drawn behind everything else (bodies, orbits, trails, axes).
The brightness of every star corresponds to its real apparent magnitude, but a star that has a name label is always drawn at least at a minimum visible brightness, so that it can be seen even if it is faint in reality (for example Sigma Octantis, magnitude about 5.4).

	Labels: the user must see not only the stars, but also their names.
   - every constellation from the list above has a name label.
   - the following stars have their own name label: the pole stars Polaris (in Ursa Minor, close to the north celestial pole) and Sigma Octantis (in Octans, close to the south celestial pole), the well-known bright stars, such as Vega, Deneb, Capella and Aldebaran, and the stars related to the determination of the path of the Sun through absolute space: lambda Herculis (near it Herschel found in 1783 the direction of the motion of the Sun, the solar apex), Vega (near the apex), Deneb (in Cygnus, the direction of the orbit of the Sun around the center of the galaxy) and Phact (alpha Columbae, in Columba, near the antapex).
   - the labels behave like the names of the body axes (see REQ-01): a label is hidden when it is behind something (for example a celestial body), and it is shown only when there is room for it.

IMPL-01
Implementation note: precision of large coordinates ("floating origin").

The absolute space reaches 950 AU from the Sun. Graphics cards compute with 32-bit numbers, which at 950 AU can store positions only in steps of about 16 000 km (more than the radius of the Earth, about 6 400 km, and much more than the radius of Pluto, about 1 200 km). Therefore everything drawn on the screen (celestial bodies, trails, orbits, grid, axes and other long lines) is computed relative to the camera or to a reference point near it, not in absolute coordinates. Only far away objects keep large coordinates; for them the error is much smaller than one pixel.
   - long straight lines (axes, grid lines, edges of the parallelepiped) are rebuilt around the camera every frame: short pieces near the camera and longer pieces (doubling in length) farther away, as in the test simulation.
   - trails and orbits are drawn relative to the camera, as in the test simulation.
