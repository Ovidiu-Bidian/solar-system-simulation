REQ-00
Terms and definitions

	[REQ-00.0.1] The following terms are used in the whole document with the meaning given here; the other requirements only use them.

Space and celestial bodies
   - [REQ-00.0.2] celestial body: the Sun, a planet or the Moon. R is the radius of a celestial body.
   - [REQ-00.0.3] reference celestial body: the celestial body around which the camera moves in "celestial body reference system" mode (the Sun, a planet or the Moon, chosen in the menu, see REQ-01.2).
   - [REQ-00.0.4] x, y, z axes and ecliptic plane: the axes of the coordinate system of absolute space and its x0y plane (see REQ-01). "Up" means towards +z and "down" towards -z; "horizontal" means parallel to the ecliptic plane and "vertical" perpendicular to it.
   - [REQ-00.0.5] body ecliptic: the plane parallel to the ecliptic plane that contains the center of the reference celestial body; its x and y axes are parallel to the x and y axes of the ecliptic, in the same direction.
   - [REQ-00.0.6] vertical axis of the celestial body: the vertical line (parallel to the z axis) through the center of the reference celestial body. It is not the rotation axis of the celestial body. It meets the surface in the two ecliptic poles of the celestial body: the north one towards +z and the south one towards -z.
   - [REQ-00.0.7] camera radius: the segment from the center of the reference celestial body to the camera. The latitude of the camera is the angle of the camera radius above (+) or below (-) the body ecliptic; its longitude is the angle of the horizontal projection of the camera radius, measured from +x towards +y. The latitude and longitude of the camera shown in the gauge and recorded in the camera log (see REQ-01.1 and REQ-05) are measured in the equatorial frame of the reference celestial body instead (x-eq, y-eq, z-eq, see REQ-01; it does not spin with the body), in every view, also in the Sun, Moon and Earth locked views (Ovi, 2026-11-13): the latitude is the angle of the camera radius above (+, north) or below (-, south) the equator; the longitude is the angle of its projection on the equator, measured from +x-eq towards +y-eq (eastwards), from 0 to 360 degrees (on the Earth, from the vernal equinox).
   - [REQ-00.0.8] Sun's path: the straight line through the center of the Sun along which the Sun moves through absolute space: towards the constellation Cygnus, at ecliptic longitude 341.5 degrees and latitude +60.6 degrees (see REQ-02). The distance behind the Sun is measured along this line, from the Sun backwards.
   - [REQ-00.0.9] absolute space: the cylinder whose axis is the Sun's path, inside which the camera moves (see REQ-01).

Camera and picture
   - [REQ-00.0.10] view direction: the direction in which the camera looks (the direction of the center of the screen).
   - [REQ-00.0.11] picture: what the camera shows on the screen. Picture up is the direction of the top edge of the screen.
   - [REQ-00.0.12] leveled picture: a picture whose top and bottom edges are parallel to the ecliptic plane. A leveled picture is upright when +z is towards the top of the screen and upside down when +z is towards the bottom of the screen.
   - [REQ-00.0.13] picture turn: the angle by which the picture is turned around the view direction, away from the leveled and upright picture with the same view direction (0 = leveled and upright, +90 = the camera turned 90 degrees to the right (clockwise, as seen from behind the camera), +180 or -180 degrees = upside down). It is shown as "turn" in the information panel (REQ-01.1).
   - [REQ-00.0.14] body place on the screen: the point of the screen where the center of the reference celestial body is shown.
   - [REQ-00.0.15] camera reference system (head reference system): the reference system of the pan/tilt head (drawn at the recorded camera in the replay, see REQ-05). Its origin is at the camera, where the pan axis and the tilt axis cross; +x_cam is the direction the head faces at pan 0 (the pan vector of the automatic view, in the plane of the head: "forward"), +y_cam is the tilt axis at pan 0, towards the left (the direction at pan +90 degrees), and +z_cam is along the pan axis, towards the top of the head (the side towards which a positive tilt raises the view direction), so that x_cam, y_cam and z_cam form a right-handed system, as the other reference systems. The view direction is turned from +x_cam towards +z_cam by the tilt: at tilt +90 degrees it points along +z_cam, at -90 degrees along -z_cam. The pan axis is that of the view: the z axis of the ecliptic in "absolute space reference system" mode and in the Sun, Moon and Earth locked views (in the ecliptic reference the axes are parallel to the x, y and z axes of the ecliptic, in the same direction); the rotation axis of the reference celestial body in the leveled pan of the pole view; the line from the center of the celestial body through the camera (outwards) in the polar pan of the pole view and the tilted meridian view (in the body center reference +x_cam and +y_cam are the +x and +y of the ecliptic turned the same way as the head, by the turn that brings the +z of the ecliptic onto the camera radius; in the pole view and the tilted meridian view they are set by the rules of the view, see REQ-03.2). Where the picture is turned by turning the plane of the pan/tilt head (see below), these axes turn with it. In this document "pan axis", "pan", "tilt axis", "tilt" and "pan vector" are always given in this system; the "forward", "backward", "left", "right", "up" and "down" of the keys are +x_cam, -x_cam, +y_cam, -y_cam, +z_cam and -z_cam.
   - [REQ-00.0.16] pan/tilt head: the observation camera works like a surveillance camera mounted on a pan/tilt head. The head turns around the pan axis and the camera turns in the head around the tilt axis. The camera never turns in its head around its own view direction: there is no "roll" between the camera and its head. In some views the picture is turned by turning the plane of the pan/tilt head (the plane perpendicular to the pan axis) around the view direction (see REQ-03.2).
   - [REQ-00.0.17] head frame: the orientation of the pan/tilt head in space, given by its pan axis: that of space in the ecliptic reference (the pan axis perpendicular to the ecliptic plane), along the camera radius in the body center reference (see below).
   - [REQ-00.0.18] pan axis: the line through the camera around which the head turns: the z_cam axis of the camera reference system. With the head frame of space it is vertical, whatever the tilt. It does not turn with the camera when it tilts, so it is not always the line through the top and bottom sides of the camera: for example at tilt +90 or -90 degrees the pan axis is the same as the view direction.
   - [REQ-00.0.19] pan: the angle of the head around the pan axis. Pan 0 means the head faces the +x_cam direction; positive pan angles turn it towards +y_cam (counterclockwise when looking from +z_cam). The pan angle is between -180 and +180 degrees; turning further past +180 degrees continues from -180 degrees, and the other way round.
   - [REQ-00.0.20] tilt axis: the line through the left and right sides of the camera, perpendicular to the pan axis and to the direction the head faces, parallel to the top and bottom edges of the screen. With the head frame of space it is horizontal.
   - [REQ-00.0.21] tilt: the angle of the camera around the tilt axis. Tilt 0 means the view direction is perpendicular to the pan axis (with the head frame of space: horizontal), in the direction the head faces, with the picture upright. Positive tilt angles raise the view direction (towards +z_cam), negative tilt angles lower it (towards -z_cam); +90 degrees is straight up and -90 degrees is straight down. Like a pan/tilt head with full tilt range, the tilt can go past the vertical: between +90 and +180 degrees the camera has tilted over the top, and between -90 and -180 degrees it has tilted under the bottom; in both cases it looks backward (away from the direction the head faces) and the picture is upside down. +180 and -180 degrees are the same orientation (looking horizontally backward, picture upside down); turning further past it continues from the other end.
   - [REQ-00.0.22] pan and tilt of the view direction: the pan and tilt that give the view direction with the head frame of space (the camera reference system parallel to the axes of the ecliptic). They are the values shown in the information panel (REQ-01.1) and written in the camera log (REQ-05), also in the body center reference; the picture turn then tells how the picture is turned.
   - [REQ-00.0.23] automatic and manual control: the keys (W/S, A/D, E/C, the arrow keys, Q/E where used) and the mouse wheel are the automatic control: they move the camera along its path in the reference system of the view, and the rules of the view set the head (its reference system, see above) and the automatic view; their directions ("forward", "backward", "left", "right", "up", "down") are those of the head reference system. The mouse is the manual control: it always grabs the image (the picture follows the mouse on both axes) and never moves the camera; it gives a manual look, relative to the automatic view, kept through the automatic movements. The final view is always the automatic view plus the manual look; it is shown as the head pan and head tilt of the view direction (the pan and tilt of the view direction in the head reference system). The "forward", "backward", "left" and "right" of the keys do not apply to the mouse. This is done in the pole view (see REQ-03.2); the other views will follow.
   - [REQ-00.0.24] core requirement of the views: in the automatic view the center of the reference celestial body keeps its place on the screen. On top of it the user can look anywhere with the mouse; the automatic movements go on, and the view is always the automatic one plus the manual look (for example, looking at the sky while the camera moves, the sky turns on the screen as the automatic view turns). This applies while the celestial body is in view (on the screen): when the manual look has taken it off the screen it may move there (swing, for example behind the view), but it is back at its place when it comes back into view (Ovi, 2026-11-25).

Reference systems of the camera
   - [REQ-00.0.25] reference plane in celestial body reference system: the equator of the reference celestial body (see "equator and rotation poles" below) in the tilted meridian view and the pole view; in the Sun locked view, a plane parallel to the ecliptic plane (the body ecliptic). Where the equator is the reference plane, the words "horizontal", "vertical", "up", "down", "latitude", "longitude", "meridian", "parallel", "ecliptic poles", "top pole", "C angle", "leveled picture" and "leveling" in this document refer to the equator and to the rotation axis.
   - [REQ-00.0.26] ecliptic reference: the head frame is that of space: the pan axis is always perpendicular to the ecliptic plane, wherever the camera is. It is used in "absolute space reference system" mode and, in "celestial body reference system" mode, by the Sun, Moon and Earth locked views.
   - [REQ-00.0.27] body center reference: the reference is a point, the center of the reference celestial body. The pan axis is always the camera radius, so the base of the head is parallel to the surface of the celestial body under the camera, and the pan vector is always parallel to that surface. It is used by the tilted meridian view (see REQ-03.2). In this reference the notions "up", "down", "forward", "backward" and "leveled picture" have no meaning; "pole view" and "view direction" are used instead.
   - [REQ-00.0.28] pan vector: the direction the head faces: in the pan plane, perpendicular to the pan axis and to the tilt axis (with the head frame of space: horizontal, the view direction projected on the ecliptic plane). Changing the pan turns it around the pan axis.
   - [REQ-00.0.29] top pole: the ecliptic pole of the celestial body that is towards the top of the screen.

Movements in "celestial body reference system" mode (see REQ-03.2)
   - [REQ-00.0.30] camera circle: the circle on which the camera moves in a meridian or parallel movement. The circle plane is the plane of the camera circle and the circle axis is the line through its center, perpendicular to the circle plane; in a movement the camera turns around the circle axis.
   - [REQ-00.0.31] meridian (real meridian): the circle centered on the center of the reference celestial body that passes through the camera and over the ecliptic poles of the celestial body (its plane is vertical and contains the camera radius). When the camera is exactly on the vertical axis of the celestial body, the meridian is the one whose plane is perpendicular to the top and bottom edges of the screen.
   - [REQ-00.0.32] parallel: the horizontal circle centered on the vertical axis of the celestial body that passes through the camera.
   - [REQ-00.0.33] tilted meridian: a circle centered on the center of the reference celestial body that passes through the camera: the real meridian, or a circle tilted from it around its line of nodes (the line where it crosses the equator) with the W/S keys, the camera riding on it, and carried with the camera by the E/C and A/D movements (see REQ-03.2).
   - [REQ-00.0.34] C angle of the tilted meridian: the tilt of the tilted meridian to the equator, from 0 to 90 degrees (90 for the real meridian, 0 for the circle in the equator). It is the same quantity as the C angle of circle C (the tilt of the camera's circle to the reference plane, see below) and has the same name (Ovi, 2026-11-13; it was called "M angle" before views_fix v_3.11; the camera log keeps its old field name, see REQ-05).
   - [REQ-00.0.35] eta and zeta: the angles of the view direction relative to the camera circle. Eta is the angle between the view direction and the circle plane. Zeta is the angle, in the circle plane, between the projection of the view direction on the circle plane and the direction from the camera to the center of the circle. When the camera looks at the center of the reference celestial body, eta and zeta are 0.
   - [REQ-00.0.36] tilted meridian view, pole view, Sun locked view and parallel movement: the tilted meridian view, the pole view and the Sun locked view are the three meridian movements (the tilted meridian view, the pole view and the Sun locked view walk on their circle with the E/C keys; the first two tilt it with W/S, the Sun locked view keeps it at a fixed angle (Ovi, 2026-11-25); the Sun locked view is built on the leveled pan of the pole view, on the body ecliptic; all three also give their own meaning to the mouse); the parallel movement is that of the A/D keys, the camera on its parallel; see REQ-03.2.
   - [REQ-00.0.37] leveling: the camera, together with its head, turns around the line from the camera to the center of the reference celestial body until the picture is leveled and upright; the body place on the screen does not change. The leveling lasts 2 seconds and turns evenly (its speed is set at its start, from the turn needed then). Where no turn around that line can make the picture leveled (the reference celestial body far from the center of the screen, the camera high above or below it), it stops at the least turned picture. When the picture is leveled, the head frame is again that of space.
   - [REQ-00.0.38] Home position: the predefined position and view direction of the camera relative to the reference celestial body (see REQ-03.2).
   - [REQ-00.0.39] equator and rotation poles: the equator is the plane through the center of the reference celestial body perpendicular to its real rotation axis (see REQ-01); the rotation poles are the two points where the axis comes out of the celestial body, the north pole given by the right-hand rule (the IAU's "positive pole"; Ovi, 2026-10-28): the pole around which the celestial body spins anticlockwise, seen from above it (the fingers of the right hand curled the way it spins, the thumb points to its north), so that every celestial body spins the same way around its own north pole. For the Sun, Mercury, the Earth, the Moon, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn and Neptune, which spin the same way as they orbit, it is the IAU's north pole; for the three that spin backwards it is the other pole, not the IAU's planetary "north" (the pole on the north side of the solar system's invariable plane): Venus RA 92.76 degrees, Dec -67.16 degrees (about 177 degrees from the ecliptic's north pole, so in the views referred to its equator its north is towards the ecliptic's south), Uranus RA 77.31 degrees, Dec +15.18 degrees, Pluto RA 132.99 degrees, Dec -6.16 degrees (the IAU's own choice for the dwarf planets, 2009). For Pluto the rotation axis is about 120 degrees from the axis of its orbit, so its equator is strongly tilted to the ecliptic plane. Where the equator is the reference plane (see "reference plane" above) they take the place of the body ecliptic and of the ecliptic poles. The longitudes on the equator are counted from x-eq (see REQ-01): from the line where the equator crosses the ecliptic plane, on the side of the node where the ecliptic rises north over the equator (for the Earth the vernal equinox), towards y-eq (eastwards, by the right-hand rule around the north pole).
   - [REQ-00.0.40] circle C: in the pole view and the Sun locked view (there to the body ecliptic, see REQ-03.2), the circle on which the camera is: centered on the center of the reference celestial body, with the distance from the camera to that center as radius, at the C angle to the equator. The C angle is signed, between -80 and +80 degrees (in the Sun, Moon and Earth locked views fixed at 38 degrees, Ovi 2026-11-25): positive when the part of C after its ascending node goes north of the equator. The line of nodes is the line where C crosses the equator; the highest point of C is its point farthest north of the equator, the lowest point the one farthest south.

REQ-01
This requirement describes the general functionality of this simulation.

	[REQ-01.0.1] The purpose of the simulation is to present the motion of some celestial bodies of our solar system (the Sun, Mercury, Venus, the Earth, the Earth's satellite the Moon, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune and Pluto) through absolute space (and their trails, see REQ-02 for details) and relative to each other.
	[REQ-01.0.2] The most important thing in this simulation are the paths of the celestial bodies through absolute space: they must be real, 100% according to the knowledge of today (2026), every number taken from a named source (JPL, IAU, Gaia / Reid & Brunthaler). What is not real (for the needs of the screen) is declared in the About window (see REQ-01.2) and never touches these paths.
	[REQ-01.0.3] The Sun moves through space (relative to the nearby stars) with about 20 km/s and through absolute space with about 230 km/s (according to current information available), around the center of the galaxy, on a circle with a radius of about 26 000 light years, one turn in about 230 million years, towards the constellation Cygnus (see REQ-02). The time frame of this simulation is only about 167 years (one revolution of Neptune, see below), so in this simulation the path of the Sun is a straight line, the Sun's path (see REQ-00).

	[REQ-01.0.4] The absolute space used in this simulation is a cylinder whose axis is the Sun's path (see REQ-00): the Sun moves around the center of the galaxy towards the constellation Cygnus, so the axis has ecliptic longitude 341.5 degrees and latitude +60.6 degrees (right ascension 313.5 degrees, declination +47.5 degrees; see REQ-02). The radius of the cylinder is 1.4 times the farthest distance of Pluto from the Sun (aphelion 49.3 AU): 69 AU. Along its axis it goes from 50 AU ahead of the Sun to 8 050 AU behind it, long enough for the trails of one revolution of Neptune (164.8 years, see REQ-02). All the planets are inside it all the time (Pluto is never farther than 49.3 AU from the Sun).
The coordinate system is the following:
   - the origin (point 0) is the center of the Sun.
   - the x0y plane is the real ecliptic plane (the plane of Earth's orbit, J2000): x towards the vernal equinox, z towards the north ecliptic pole. The ecliptic plane is the reference plane of "absolute space reference system" mode; the axis of the cylinder is tilted by 60.6 degrees to it, so the trails leave it at this angle.
	[REQ-01.0.5] The camera that observes the motion of the celestial bodies can be moved only inside the cylinder (the way the camera is positioned/moved is specified in REQ-03). When the camera reaches its wall (the round side or one of the two ends) it stops there: any movement that would take it outside is blocked (movements along the wall or away from it remain possible).
	[REQ-01.0.6] The ecliptic plane has one grid, made of squares with sides of 4 AU (the grid lines at multiples of 4 AU), drawn only inside the ellipse in which the ecliptic plane cuts the cylinder; the ellipse is drawn as the edge of the grid. There is no other grid (none along the trails). The outline of the cylinder (its two end circles and 4 lines along it) and its axis (the Sun's path; behind the Sun the Sun's own trail runs on it) are drawn as thin lines. The "H" key hides the markings of the absolute space and shows them again (at the start they are shown): the grid (with its edge), the orbits of the celestial bodies, the names of the axes of the coordinate system ("+x", "-x", ...) and the outline of the cylinder with its axis; then only the Universe is seen: the celestial bodies (with the axes of the reference celestial body), their trails, the stars, the constellations and the Milky Way.
	[REQ-01.0.7] The names of the axes of the coordinate system ("+x", "-x", "+y", "-y", "+z", "-z") are written where these axes leave the cylinder. "+x", "-x", "+y" and "-y" are centred exactly above their axis (below it when the camera is below the ecliptic plane). These names are behind the celestial bodies: a celestial body that passes in front of a name (nearer to the camera) covers it, wholly or in part, as it covers the lines.
	[REQ-01.0.8] The Sun stays in point 0 and does not move. The bodies are lit by the Sun: the light comes from the center of the Sun, with the same strength at every distance. The shadow of the Moon on the Earth (eclipses of the Sun) and the shadow of the Earth on the Moon (eclipses of the Moon) are real, with umbra and penumbra: for every point of the surface, the part of the disk of the Sun covered by the other body is computed from the real sizes and places (also when a body is drawn larger or smaller than real, see REQ-03.2). On the Earth the umbra, where the Sun is wholly covered, is black (also the faint light of its dark side, see below, is gone there), and the penumbra goes from the lit surface to black at the edge of the umbra; its night side keeps its faint light (Ovi, 2026-11-18). So that it can be seen, the black umbra is drawn twice its real size (radius), the penumbra going from its real outer edge to that black disc; in reality the umbra is only about 1 - 4% of the radius of the whole dark spot (the penumbra; some 100 - 270 km wide, the most with the Moon at perigee and the Sun at aphelion), so it is drawn at most about 8% of it; this is done only while the real umbra reaches the Earth (otherwise only the penumbra is seen), and it is declared in the About window (Ovi, 2026-11-18; twice the real size, Ovi 2026-11-22: it was at least 20% of the whole spot). In the shadow of the Earth the Moon is copper red: it is lit by the sunlight that the atmosphere of the Earth bends into the shadow (mostly red); its brightness is shown as the eye sees it, adapted to the dark (in reality some 10 000 times fainter than the full Moon; this is declared in the About window). Their dark side is lit a little, as the night side of the Earth is lit by a full Moon (a faint, slightly bluish light from all directions), so that its surface can still be seen; the lit side stays clearly brighter.
	[REQ-01.0.9] All planets move relative to the Sun on their real Keplerian orbits, and the Moon around the Earth (see REQ-02). (Ovi, 2026-11-18: the names of the orbits written in their lines, described here before, were never in the simulation; taken out.)

	[REQ-01.0.10] The reference celestial body displays its rotation axis as the z-eq axis of its equatorial frame (see below), oriented in its real direction in space (its north pole by the right-hand rule, see REQ-00.0.39; the directions from the IAU's report on the rotational elements). The body spins around this axis (see REQ-02).
	[REQ-01.0.11] The celestial bodies are shown with real surface maps, made from spacecraft and telescope images (for example from NASA). Where a body has never been completely photographed, the missing part is filled in so that it matches the photographed surface around it (for example the southern part of Pluto, south of about -30 degrees latitude, is filled in with the average color of the nearest photographed surface, blended smoothly into the photo, with a fine painted pattern). The sources of the maps are shown in the program and are written in their files (see REQ-01.0.25). The Sun has no map: its surface is drawn by the program (see REQ-02).
	[REQ-01.0.12] When the mouse is over a celestial body on the screen (on its outline, or less than 6 pixels from it, so that small or far bodies, seen as dots, can also be pointed at), the name of the body is written in a small box just below and to the right of the mouse (on the other side near the edges of the screen); while the zoom screen is on, the box is in the lower right corner of the zoom screen, so that it does not cover the target, and it lists all the bodies whose disc, as drawn in the zoom screen, touches its focus frame. When there are several bodies under the mouse, their names are listed one under the other, left aligned, in the order of the menu (from the Sun outwards, the Moon after the Earth). A body hidden by another body is not listed. In the zoom screen the list is renewed 4 times a second, as the information panel's values. In the zoom screen the box lists, after the bodies, also the constellations seen in the zoom screen and the named stars (see REQ-04) inside its focus frame, as the bodies, whose names are not written over it: first the constellations (one of their lines or the place of their name is in the zoom screen), then the stars, from the brightest (a star hidden by a celestial body is not listed). Nothing is shown while the mouse drags the picture or is over a panel or the menu.
	[REQ-01.0.13] Zoom screen: the "T" key switches the zoom screen on or off, in both camera modes. It works like the screen of a digital camera with a zoom lens: a rectangle centred on the mouse, with the same shape as the screen and 40% of its width and height (always inside the screen with the camera's body around it: near the edges of the screen it stops there, and it still shows the area under the mouse at its center), shows the area under it larger, as it is drawn on the screen (bodies drawn larger than real are shown larger as well, so that, for example, the phases of Venus can be seen from the Earth): 7.6 times each time it is switched on; while the zoom screen is on, the "+" key (also "=") raises the zoom to the next value and the "-" key lowers it to the previous one, among 4, 5.5, 7.6, 10.5, 14.5 and 20 times (5 equal steps). In the zoom screen the Sun's minimum apparent radius is an angle equal to 8% of the zoom screen's height at 4 times, so it grows with the zoom: at 20 times it is 40% (a disc of 80% of the height) in the middle of the screen; towards the edges of the screen the picture is stretched, as with a wide-angle lens, and the disc is larger there. The minimum apparent radius of the planets is 30% of the Sun's minimum (instead of 50%), and the Moon's minimum is half of the planets' minimum in the zoom screen. The picture in it is drawn again for the narrower field, sharp, not with enlarged pixels, and the lines (trails, orbits, grid, axes) keep their width in pixels. The Milky Way is shown in it at 20% of its brightness (see REQ-04). The stars are magnified in it: the brightest star's radius is 60% of the Moon's minimum apparent radius in the zoom screen (so it grows with the zoom), the other stars in proportion (their brightness kept), and no star is smaller than on the screen; the gap between a constellation line and its stars (2 pixels on the screen, see REQ-04) grows with the zoom too: 2 pixels at 4 times, 10 pixels at 20 times. Around it there is the dark body of the camera (6 pixels wide); over the picture, as on a camera's screen, there are white corner brackets, a focus frame at the mouse (only its corners are drawn), the zoom (for example "4x") in the upper left corner and a W-T bar (wide - telephoto) at the bottom. The names written over the picture are not shown over the zoom screen and its frame, except the names written along the axes of the reference celestial body: the zoom screen has its own, along its magnified axes, placed by the same rule on the part of each axis seen in it (with their gaps in the line). The zoom screen follows the mouse; while it is on, the mouse is captured by the program (it cannot leave the picture: its movements move the zoom screen's target, kept within the picture, 10 times slower while the Shift key is held, to aim it precisely; the browser says once how to free it, Esc, which also turns the zoom screen off) and the mouse pointer is not shown over the picture (it would cover the target; the focus frame shows where the mouse is), nor, in "absolute space reference system" mode, the little cross in the middle of the screen (see REQ-03.1); the mouse still moves the camera as usual, and while it drags the picture the zoom screen stays where the drag started.
	[REQ-01.0.14] In "celestial body reference system" mode the reference celestial body displays two sets of short axes through its center, its equatorial plane and its ecliptic grid. They are not displayed on the other celestial bodies, nor in "absolute space reference system" mode:

Information panel

	[REQ-01.1.1] The information panel is in the upper right part of the screen (it can be restrained, see below). It is compact (340 pixels wide, its lines close together). Its values (positions, distances, angles, speeds) are written 4 times a second, which is enough to read them and saves the program's time; its first line and the marks in the menu change at once. It shows:
   - [REQ-01.1.2] on the first line, what was selected in the menu (see REQ-01.2): in "celestial body reference system" mode the reference celestial body and its view, for example "Neptune - pole view", "Pluto - Sun locked" or "Earth - Moon locked"; in "absolute space reference system" mode "Absolute space". Under it, in one row: the simulation speed buttons "-", "Start/Stop", the direction of time and "+" and the current simulation speed (see below), a group of constant width at the left, and at the right end of the row the reference axes button (see below).
   - [REQ-01.1.3] the speed of the body/camera (depending on the selected reference system) in absolute space (in km/s) (extremely important, this is one of the main topics of this simulation): the size of the sum of its two velocities, its velocity on its orbit (for the Moon, its velocity around the Earth together with the velocity of the Earth around the Sun) and the velocity of the solar system through absolute space (the velocity of the Sun, along the Sun's path). So it is between v_abs - v_orbit and v_abs + v_orbit (v_abs: the speed of the Sun in absolute space, v_orbit: the speed of the body on its orbit); for the Sun it is v_abs. While the motion is stopped it is 0.00 km/s: no simulated time passes and the body does not move (Ovi, 2026-11-15). It is shown with 2 decimals, in the color of the first line, as "Speed in space". In "absolute space reference system" mode this row shows instead the speed of the camera in absolute space ("Cam speed", see below).
   - [REQ-01.1.4] the position of the observation camera in absolute space (x, y, z) in AU, with 2 decimals. When the camera is closer than 0.01 AU to the center of the reference celestial body, its position relative to the center of that body is also displayed in km ("From <body> x, y, z"; with these small distances the values in AU would hardly change). The three values are one space apart.
   - [REQ-01.1.5] the pan and tilt of the view direction, in degrees (see REQ-00); when the picture turn is not 0, also the picture turn ("turn"). They are the direction of the view (the center of the screen) in the ecliptic coordinates of absolute space (J2000): the pan is the ecliptic longitude, from the +x axis (the J2000 equinox), the tilt the ecliptic latitude (+90 degrees = +z, the ecliptic's north pole), and "turn" the picture's turn from level to the ecliptic. They are the same in every view, also on the celestial bodies (Ovi, 2026-11-13; this is written in the About window). What changes with the view is what the picture is kept level to, and so where the look up and down stops (see the table under this list). In the Sun, Moon and Earth locked views the row always shows the pan and tilt. On the Earth, in the leveled pan of the pole view, while the camera is closer than 30 Earth radii to the center of the Earth, the row shows instead the equatorial astronomical coordinates of the view direction, "RA / Dec (J2000)": the right ascension in hours and minutes of time (to 0.1 minute) and the declination in degrees and minutes of arc (to 1 minute), as precise as one pixel of mouse movement turns the view (see REQ-03.2). For the stars these are their catalogue coordinates; for the Sun, the Moon and the planets they are their direction as seen from the camera (topocentric), which can differ from the geocentric coordinates given by the almanacs (from the center of the Earth): for the Moon by up to about 1 degree at the Earth's surface and up to about 30 degrees at 30 Earth radii. While the row shows RA / Dec, the little cross of absolute space marks the view direction in the middle of the screen (see REQ-03.1). A small button next to "RA / Dec" switches between the J2000 coordinates ("J2000") and the J2000 coordinates adjusted to today's date ("adj. J2000"; the date in its tooltip), read from the computer's clock (Ovi, 2026-11-11): the J2000 direction turned by the precession of the Earth's axis from J2000.0 to that date (IAU 1976 precession angles), the mean coordinates of date; in 2026 they differ from J2000 by about 1.3 minutes of right ascension for most stars and up to 8 arc minutes of declination, much more near the poles (Polaris about 35 minutes of right ascension). The nutation (up to 17 arc seconds) and the proper motions of the stars, which the almanacs add for the apparent place, are not included, nor the stellar aberration (up to 20.5 arc seconds), which can be shown magnified in a row of its own (see REQ-01.1.8 and REQ-04.0.9) (this is written in the About window); together they can change the last digit of the declination shown. It is only a display: the simulation's sky and the Earth's axis stay in J2000. The program starts with J2000.
   - [REQ-01.1.6] the speed of the camera in absolute space ("Cam speed", Ovi, 2026-11-15): the magnitude (modulus) of the camera's velocity vector in absolute space, without a sign (the picture shows where the camera goes). It is the sum of two parts: the camera's own moves made in real time (the user's moves, keys pressed together adding up as vectors, see REQ-03.0.14; the way to Home; what the menu and the mouse change), relative to what carries it (in "absolute space reference system" mode the Sun, in "celestial body reference system" mode the center of the reference celestial body), per second of real time (its moves over the last 0.3 seconds divided by that time: a steady move shows exactly its speed, and the speed is 0 that long after the camera stopped); and, while the motion runs, the velocity of what carries the camera (the Sun's, 230 km/s along the Sun's path, or the reference celestial body's in absolute space, see above) together with the camera's moves made by the simulated time (the turn of the Sun, Moon and Earth locked views with their target, the pushes of the moving bodies, the stops at the wall of the space while the reference celestial body moves on), per second of simulated time; both reversed while time runs backward (views_fix v_3.16, Ovi 2026-11-15: in v_3.14 and v_3.15 the moves made by the simulated time were counted per second of real time, and a camera left alone in the Pluto Sun locked view showed 205 to 424 km/s instead of about 230). While the motion is stopped no simulated time passes and nothing moves but the camera: only its own moves count. So a camera that is not moved shows 230 km/s in "absolute space reference system" mode, the speed in space of the reference celestial body in "celestial body reference system" mode (in every view), and 0.00 km/s while the motion is stopped. It is shown in km/s with 2 decimals below the speed of light (299 792.458 km/s), above it in AU/s with 2 decimals (below 1 AU/s with 3 significant digits). In "absolute space reference system" mode it is the first row, in place of the speed in absolute space.

REQ-02
Bodies, orbits, time scale and trails

	[REQ-02.0.1] The simulation shows the Sun, the planets Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune and Pluto, and the Earth's satellite, the Moon.
   - [REQ-02.0.2] the Sun: real radius (695 700 km), in point 0, it does not move. It is self-lit, with a soft glow. Its surface is drawn by the program (Ovi, 2026-10-30; there is no real map of a surface that changes all the time): only its look, in screen time, as the spin of the bodies (see below): it stops when the motion is stopped, runs backward when time runs backward and never touches the paths of the celestial bodies (this is declared in the About window, see REQ-01.2). Its edge is darker and redder than its center (limb darkening). Seen close (or in the zoom screen, see REQ-01) its surface is made of granules: small bright cells with dark lanes between them, slowly changing. Sunspots: a dark, slightly red center (umbra) in a gray rim of radial threads (penumbra), up to about 3 degrees of the Sun in radius; they come in groups of two (the leading spot nearer to the equator, the following one behind it and a little nearer to the pole), only in the real bands between 5 and 35 degrees north and south (most near 15 degrees); a group is born, grows in about 15% of its life and then fades; it lives about 20 to 60 seconds; at most 10 groups at the same time on the whole Sun. Around the groups there are bright mottled patches (faculae), seen mostly near the edge. Being gas, the Sun turns faster at its equator than near its poles (real: about 25 days at the equator, about 35 days near the poles): its equator turns once in 10 seconds, as the other bodies, its poles about 40% slower; every spot moves with the speed of its latitude.
   - [REQ-02.0.3] the explosions of the Sun (Ovi, 2026-10-30; also only their look, in screen time, as its surface): flares: two bright white ribbons flash up between the two spots of a group (about 1 second to the peak, then they fade in about 6 seconds), at most two in the life of a group; prominences: arcades of thin glowing loops of gas standing on the surface (about 30 000 to 130 000 km high, at any latitude up to 65 degrees, turning with the surface), along which knots of gas flow; beyond the edge of the disc they glow pink (as in photos taken through a hydrogen-alpha filter or in a total eclipse), in front of the disc they are seen as long dark threads (filaments); each lives about 20 to 60 seconds, and about a third of them erupt at the end of their life: they rise, swell and fly off; coronal mass ejections (CMEs): every erupting prominence sends out a faint white bubble, brightest at its edge, that grows from the Sun to about 15 Sun radii in 12 seconds and fades. The explosions have their real size, so they are seen only near the Sun or in the zoom screen. At the start the Sun is calm (Ovi, 2026-11-26): no explosion (flare, erupting prominence, CME) begins in its first 4 seconds of screen time; one that would have begun then does not happen at all (a prominence then ends calmly), so none appears half way through.
   - [REQ-02.0.4] the planets: real radius, real rotation axis (north pole direction from IAU; for Venus and Uranus, which turn backwards, the pole used is the one around which the planet turns counterclockwise seen from above it, its positive pole, opposite to the one IAU calls north: Ovi, 2026-11-18) and real surface map (for Venus its cloud cover, which is what is seen of it from space). They move on their real orbits around the Sun (JPL approximate Keplerian elements, J2000 ecliptic: semi-major axis, eccentricity, inclination, longitude of the ascending node, longitude of perihelion). The motion follows Kepler's laws (faster near the Sun), each planet independently (the planets do not attract each other).
   - [REQ-02.0.5] Saturn also has its main rings (C, B and A, 74 658 to 136 775 km from its center), in the plane of its equator, with their real map (Solar System Scope). They are lit by the Sun, so their side turned away from the Sun is dark. The faint rings of Jupiter, Uranus and Neptune are not drawn (this is declared in the About window, see REQ-01.2).
   - [REQ-02.0.6] the Moon: real radius, real surface map; it moves on its real orbit around the Earth (it travels together with the Earth around the Sun): the mean lunar elements (the node of its orbit turns once in 18.6 years, its perigee once in 8.85 years) and the main periodic terms of its longitude, latitude and distance caused by the Sun (Meeus, Astronomical Algorithms, from ELP-2000/82), within about 2 arc minutes (some 200 km) of its real place. Its rotation axis follows Cassini's laws (its equator tilted 1.543 degrees to the ecliptic) and it always shows the same face to the Earth. Its orbit is drawn as its path around the Earth over one month (from half a month before to half a month after the current time).
   - [REQ-02.0.7] the planets have the same minimum apparent radius (about 0.17 degrees), the Moon half of it and the Sun twice it (see REQ-03.2), so from big distances the planets are seen as dots of the same size, the Moon a little smaller and the Sun as the biggest dot. (The smallest dot: see REQ-03.2.4.)
   - [REQ-02.0.8] far away, more than 400 AU behind the Sun (see REQ-00, Sun's path), only the trails show the solar system, converging to a point: the celestial bodies are drawn at their real size (no minimum apparent radius), the orbits and the axes of the reference celestial body are not drawn.
   - [REQ-02.0.9] the orbit of every planet (and of the Moon around the Earth) is drawn as a thin line in the color of the body (Pluto light orange, Earth blue, the Moon gray, Mars red, Jupiter tan, Saturn pale gold, Uranus light cyan, Neptune blue, Mercury gray, Venus pale yellow). Close to a body the line passes exactly through its center.
   - [REQ-02.0.10] at the start every planet is in the ecliptic plane, at the ascending node of its orbit (where it crosses the ecliptic going up). These are not the real positions of the planets on a given date but this doesn't matter for the scope of simulation.

	[REQ-02.0.11] Time scale: the time of the simulation is measured in Earth hours, days, weeks, months and years. The simulation speed (chosen with the "-" and "+" buttons, see REQ-01.1) says how much Earth time passes in one real second: 1 hour, 12 hours, 1, 3 days, 1, 2, 3 weeks, 1, 3, 4, 6 months or 1 year. The orbits and the trails use this time scale; the real orbital periods are: Mercury about 0.24 years, Venus about 0.62 years, Earth 1 year, Mars about 1.88 years, Jupiter about 11.9 years, Saturn about 29.5 years, Uranus about 84 years, Neptune about 165 years, Pluto about 248 years; the Moon goes around the Earth in about 27.3 days. So one orbit and the trails take:
   - 1 hour/sec: the Moon about 11 minutes, Mercury about 35 minutes, Venus about 1.5 hours, Earth about 2.4 hours, Jupiter about 29 hours, Pluto about 25 days; the trails move with about 0.0055 AU/sec and reach the end of the absolute space in about 17 days.
   - 12 hours/sec: the Moon about 55 seconds, Mercury about 2.9 minutes, Venus about 7.5 minutes, Earth about 12 minutes, Jupiter about 2.4 hours, Pluto about 50 hours; the trails move with about 0.066 AU/sec and reach the end of the absolute space in about 34 hours.
   - 1 day/sec: the Moon about 27 seconds, Mercury about 88 seconds, Venus about 3.7 minutes, Earth about 6.1 minutes, Jupiter about 72 minutes, Pluto about 25 hours; the trails move with about 0.13 AU/sec and reach the end of the absolute space in about 17 hours.
   - 3 days/sec: the Moon about 9.1 seconds, Mercury about 29 seconds, Venus about 75 seconds, Earth about 2 minutes, Jupiter about 24 minutes, Pluto about 8.4 hours; the trails move with about 0.40 AU/sec and reach the end of the absolute space in about 5.6 hours.
   - 1 week/sec: the Moon about 3.9 seconds, Mercury about 12.6 seconds, Venus about 32 seconds, Earth about 52 seconds, Jupiter about 10.3 minutes, Pluto about 3.6 hours; the trails move with about 0.93 AU/sec and reach the end of the absolute space in about 2.4 hours.
   - 2 weeks/sec: the Moon about 2 seconds, Mercury about 6.3 seconds, Venus about 16 seconds, Earth about 26 seconds, Jupiter about 5.2 minutes, Pluto about 1.8 hours; the trails move with about 1.86 AU/sec and reach the end of the absolute space in about 73 minutes.
   - 3 weeks/sec: the Moon about 1.3 seconds, Mercury about 4.2 seconds, Venus about 10.7 seconds, Earth about 17 seconds, Jupiter about 3.4 minutes, Pluto about 1.2 hours; the trails move with about 2.8 AU/sec and reach the end of the absolute space in about 48 minutes.
   - 1 month/sec: Earth 12 seconds, Jupiter about 2.4 minutes, Pluto about 50 minutes; the trails move with about 4.0 AU/sec and reach the end of the absolute space in about 33 minutes.
   - 3 months/sec: Earth 4 seconds, Jupiter about 48 seconds, Pluto about 16.5 minutes; the trails move with about 12.1 AU/sec and reach the end of the absolute space in about 11 minutes.
   - 4 months/sec: Earth 3 seconds, Jupiter about 36 seconds, Pluto about 12.4 minutes; the trails move with about 16.2 AU/sec and reach the end of the absolute space in about 8.3 minutes.
   - 6 months/sec: Earth 2 seconds, Jupiter about 24 seconds, Pluto about 8.3 minutes; the trails move with about 24.3 AU/sec and reach the end of the absolute space in about 5.6 minutes.
   - 1 year/sec: Earth 1 second, Jupiter about 12 seconds, Pluto about 4.1 minutes; the trails move with about 48.5 AU/sec and reach the end of the absolute space in about 2.8 minutes.
The shape of the trails does not depend on the simulation speed; only how fast they are formed changes.
	[REQ-02.0.12] The spin of the bodies is not on this time scale: the real rotation speeds cannot be followed by the human eye anyway, the spin only needs to show that the body turns around its rotation axis. So every body turns once in 10 seconds, at every simulation speed, in its real direction around its real rotation axis. Exception: in the eclipse views (REQ-03.2.42, REQ-03.2.47) the Earth turns at its real speed adjusted to the simulation speed. The Sun is gas and turns faster at its equator: its equator turns once in 10 seconds, its poles about 40% slower (see above). Exception: the Moon always shows the same face to the Earth, so it turns once around its axis in every orbit around the Earth (about 27.3 days), on the time scale of the orbits.

	[REQ-02.0.13] Trails: the bodies stay in this fixed space, but every body leaves behind a trail: a yellow line, 2 pixels wide. Every point of a trail is created where the body is at that moment (x, y, z) and then moves backwards along the Sun's path (see REQ-00), with the speed of the Sun through space. In this way the trails show the real paths of the bodies through absolute space: the Sun leaves a straight line, the axis of the absolute space, and every planet a slanted spiral around it, leaving the ecliptic plane at 60.6 degrees (the orbits of the planets are not perpendicular to the Sun's path); the Moon leaves a spiral around the Earth's (drawn compressed, see below).
	[REQ-02.0.14] The speed of the Sun through space is its real speed around the center of the galaxy, about 230 km/s, towards the constellation Cygnus: ecliptic longitude 341.5 degrees, latitude +60.6 degrees (the galactic rotation, towards galactic longitude 90 degrees, plus the Sun's own motion among the nearby stars, U, V, W = 11.1, 12.24, 7.25 km/s, Schoenrich, Binney & Dehnen 2010: galactic longitude 87.4 degrees, latitude +1.7 degrees). It uses the same time scale as the orbits: the Sun travels about 48.5 AU in one Earth year (about 12 000 AU in one Pluto orbit); the speed of the trails on the screen depends on the simulation speed (see above). One loop of a spiral is then about 12 AU long for Mercury, 30 AU for Venus, 49 AU for Earth, 91 AU for Mars, 575 AU for Jupiter, 1 430 AU for Saturn, 4 080 AU for Uranus, 8 000 AU for Neptune and 12 000 AU for Pluto; one loop of the Moon around the Earth about 4 AU.
	[REQ-02.0.15] The Moon's trail (a visual aid, like the plumes): its real path is a spiral around the Earth's trail so stretched (one loop about 3.6 AU long and 0.0026 AU wide) that near the Earth it would be only a short line beside it. It is drawn as a compressed spiral around the Earth's trail: it starts 2 radii from the center of the Moon, with a plume, like the other trails (see below), and goes back along the Earth's trail, 800 Earth-Moon distances per loop (instead of 3.6 AU), one loop per sidereal month, around it in the real directions of the Moon from the Earth at each moment and at their real distance, shrinking evenly to the Earth's trail over an eighth of a loop (3.4 days), where it ends, 100 Earth-Moon distances behind the Moon. It is seen only near the Earth: fully within 10 Earth-Moon distances, not at all beyond 20, fading between; in the zoom screen the distance counts divided by the zoom, and from 7.6 times on it is seen at any distance, with its width and the length of its loops enlarged as the distance of the Moon from the Earth is there (see REQ-03.2), so that it starts from the drawn Moon. The graphics card draws it from the Moon's places, each computed once.
	[REQ-02.0.16] A trail ends at the back end of the absolute space, 8 050 AU behind the Sun; a point needs about 167 Earth years (one revolution of Neptune) to get there from the Sun (for the real time, see the time scale above). So the trail of Neptune shows one complete loop of its spiral and the trail of Pluto about two thirds of one. The trails are smooth also for the fast planets: a planet leaves a point every 2 degrees of its orbit, also between two pictures on the screen (Mercury and Venus, the fastest, a point about every 2 Earth days, so that their whole trails can be kept; near the camera their pieces are split, so that they stay smooth).
	[REQ-02.0.17] Start of the trail: at every distance the trail of a body starts 2 radii from its center (1 radius above its surface), so that the body looks as if it moved away from its trail at a huge speed. All these distances are measured in radii of the body as it is drawn: far away, where it is drawn larger than real (see the minimum apparent radius above), and in the Moon and Earth locked views, where the other body is drawn larger or smaller than real (see REQ-03.2), they grow or shrink with it, so the trail and its plume look the same in every view.
	[REQ-02.0.18] Plume: when the camera is closer than 80 radii (of the body as drawn, see above) to the center of a body (any body, the Sun included), the first part of its trail looks like the trail of a plane's engine, showing the energy and the speed of the body: yellow "smoke", 0.3 radii thick and 80 radii long, made of soft puffs that flow away from the body very fast (37.5 radii per second, like high-pressure steam leaving an engine; only while the motion is running). This is only a visual effect: at these distances the real trail is a straight line, made in a fraction of a second. The plume appears gradually: it starts to be seen at 80 radii and is complete at 40 radii, while the line it replaces fades out at the same time. From a quarter of its length the smoke gets thinner, down to the width of the line, and in its last part it fades out while the line under it fades in, so that at the end of the plume only the line remains, exactly as bright as the rest of the trail: there is no visible point where the plume ends and the line begins.

REQ-03
This requirement describes the control of the observation camera by keyboard and mouse.

	[REQ-03.0.1] The observation camera is mounted on a pan/tilt head (see REQ-00). Its angle of view is 50 degrees vertically (from the top to the bottom of the window), like the comfortable field of view of the eye (about a 25 mm lens on a full-frame camera); horizontally it follows the shape of the window (for example 73.5 degrees in a 16:10 window, 79.3 degrees in a 16:9 one), so that nothing is stretched. The minimum apparent sizes of the celestial bodies (see REQ-02) are angles in this view.
	[REQ-03.0.2] In "absolute space reference system" mode the pan/tilt head is always leveled, wherever the camera is: the picture is leveled, upright when the tilt is between -90 and +90 degrees and upside down otherwise. The picture is turned (it is not leveled) only:
   - [REQ-03.0.3] in the tilted meridian view, at any time (body center reference, see REQ-03.2)
   - [REQ-03.0.4] in the Sun, Moon and Earth locked views: only during the turn of the picture entering the view, until it is leveled (see REQ-03.2.36); never upside down (Ovi, 2026-11-25; before, also south up and during the 180-degree turn between north up and south up)
   - [REQ-03.0.5] during the 1 second turn of an upside-down picture when switching to "absolute space reference system" mode (see below).
	[REQ-03.0.6] When the picture is upright, the user can move the tilt with the mouse only in the range -90 to +90 degrees. This is mandatory because a tilted ecliptic on the screen, and worse than that, a tilted ecliptic while upside down, makes the user completely lose orientation in space.
	[REQ-03.0.7] The tilt can be carried over +90 or -90 degrees only by a meridian movement in "celestial body reference system" mode (see REQ-03.2), never by the user with the mouse. In "absolute space reference system" mode the picture is always upright (tilt between -90 and +90 degrees).
In the tilted meridian view and the pole view the mouse gives the manual look, at most 90 degrees up or down from the automatic view (see REQ-03.2).
Examples: pan 0, tilt 0: looking horizontally along +x. Pan 90, tilt 0: looking horizontally along +y. Pan 0, tilt -60: looking towards +x and 60 degrees down. Pan 0, tilt +120: the camera has tilted over the top; it looks towards -x and 60 degrees up, with the picture upside down (this can happen only in "celestial body reference system" mode, with a meridian movement).
	[REQ-03.0.8] The camera angles are always kept within fixed limits, also internally in the program, no matter how long the camera keeps turning or moving: the "pan" and "tilt" angles are always between -180 and +180 degrees. Every other angle used to describe the position or the orientation of the camera or of the celestial bodies (for example the rotation angle of a celestial body around its own axis) is also kept within a limited range (for example -180 to +180 or 0 to 360 degrees), so that no value grows without limit.

	[REQ-03.0.9] The program starts (behind the intro, see REQ-01.3) in the Sun locked view (see REQ-03.2) of the reference celestial body Pluto, at 3 months/sec, from the picture chosen by Ovi (2026-11-25, so that the Sun stays on the screen all the way round Pluto's orbit): the camera at (43.71, 1.67, -12.82) AU, (6 749, -14 495, 4 593) km from the center of Pluto (16 636 km, 14*R; 16.0 degrees above Pluto's ecliptic), pan 150.1 degrees, tilt -2.5 degrees, turn 0 degrees, circle C at 38 degrees through the camera (as always in this view). At the start Pluto is at another place on its orbit, so the picture is kept relative to the direction of the Sun, as the Sun locked view keeps it (see REQ-03.2): the camera's place around Pluto, the view and circle C are turned around the z axis of the ecliptic by the change of the Sun's direction sideways (about 109.5 degrees); Pluto keeps its place on the screen (in the lower right part) and the Sun its angle sideways (32.1 degrees to the left of the view). The Sun is at its real height at the start (0.4 degrees below Pluto's ecliptic; in the picture 16.3 degrees above), so it is about 17 degrees lower on the screen than in the picture, at the left, a little above the middle (in a window of 1256 x 708 pixels at x 152, y 327); as Pluto goes round its orbit it swings up and down (+/-17.1 degrees, the inclination of the orbit), always on the screen: from 68% of the half-height below the center of the screen to 89% above it. (Before 2026-11-25: Ovi's picture of 2026-11-11, the camera at (-5.73, -29.77, 4.84) AU, pan 47.0 degrees, tilt -3.6 degrees, circle C at 58.9 degrees; two pictures tried on 2026-11-25 left the Sun off the screen at its highest.) The picture appears gradually when the program starts: from black to full brightness in 2 seconds.
	[REQ-03.0.10] The reference system is chosen in the menu (see REQ-01.2). When the mode is switched, the camera keeps its position and its view direction. When switching from "celestial body reference system" mode to "absolute space reference system" mode while the picture is upside down, the camera keeps its position and its view direction and the picture turns upright smoothly, around the center of the screen, in 1 second (not instantly, because a sudden turn confuses the user). The tilt is brought back between -90 and +90 degrees and the pan changes by 180 degrees, so that the view direction stays the same (for example pan 0, tilt +120 becomes pan 180, tilt +60). A south-up picture (see REQ-03.0.4), turned 180 degrees around the view direction with its tilt between -90 and +90 degrees, is leveled instead: it turns upright in 2 seconds, as every leveling of the picture (Ovi, 2026-11-18). When switching from the tilted meridian view, the picture is leveled (see REQ-00) and the head frame is again that of space; the tilted meridian is set back to the real meridian (C angle 90 degrees).
When switching to "absolute space reference system" mode with a turned picture, the picture always ends leveled and upright: it turns around the view direction (the view direction stays), also when the mouse moves while it turns.

	[REQ-03.0.11] The mouse controls the camera only while the left mouse button is held down ("drag"). While the button is held down the mouse cursor is not visible on the screen; it appears again when the button is released.
	[REQ-03.0.12] The mouse sensitivity is 0.05 degrees of "pan" or "tilt" angle per pixel of mouse movement (the movement is the one reported by the operating system, so the pointer speed settings of the operating system also influence it). While the Shift key is held, the mouse is 10 times finer (0.005 degrees per pixel), to aim the view precisely (for example at a star, to read its right ascension and declination, see REQ-01.1). The first two movements reported after the pointer is locked (at the press of the left button, and when the zoom screen opens), and any single movement of more than half the window, are ignored: browsers sometimes report a large false movement then (Ovi, 2026-11-19). Moving the mouse "forward" means moving it away from the user (the cursor would go towards the top of the screen), moving it "backward" means moving it towards the user.
	[REQ-03.0.13] The mouse works at any time, also while a movement key is held, in both camera modes and in every movement: the view changed with the mouse is kept, and the movement goes on from the new view, on the same camera circle. Also while the picture turns to a view just entered (see REQ-03.2.14, REQ-03.2.22): the mouse does not end that turn, which goes on, the look moving with the turning picture (Ovi, 2026-11-19: a drag then made the picture jump up to 119 degrees at once); only during the 180-degree turns between north up and south up, while the picture turns and the camera comes to circle C entering the Sun locked view (REQ-03.2.36) and on the way to Home nothing is controlled (see REQ-03.2, REQ-03.2.41).

	[REQ-03.0.14] The speed of the moves of the camera made by the user (per second of real time; its speed in absolute space, shown in the information panel, is another one, see REQ-01.1) depends on its distance from the center of the nearest celestial body (the Sun, a planet or the Moon). The speed of one move of the camera (one key or the mouse wheel; moves made together add up as vectors: two moves at right angles (W and D at tilt 0) give 1.41 times this speed, three (W, D and PgUp) 1.73 times, Ovi, 2026-11-15; see "Cam speed", REQ-01.1) is:
   - distance from 5 million km to 0.5 AU: 0.4 AU/sec
   - distance from 0.5 AU to 1 AU: 1 AU/sec
   - distance from 1 AU to 2 AU: 2 AU/sec
   - distance from 2 AU to 4 AU: 4 AU/sec
   - distance from 4 AU to 8 AU: 8 AU/sec
   - distance from 8 AU to 12 AU: 10 AU/sec
   - distance greater than 12 AU: 12 AU/sec (the maximum speed of the camera)
   - distance smaller than 5 million km: the speed is half of the current distance per second (for example 2 500 000 km/sec at 5 million km, 2 377 km/sec at 4 753 km). In this way the camera slows down when approaching the celestial body and never jumps over it. With the minimum apparent size of the celestial bodies (see REQ-02), this limit is noticed mainly near the Sun: the sudden drop of the speed shows that the camera is close to it.
The camera never moves, in one picture on the screen, more than half of the distance to the center of the nearest celestial body, so that also a slow picture (a computer that is busy for a moment) cannot make the camera jump over a celestial body.
In "absolute space reference system" mode, more than 60 AU (twice the distance of Neptune from the Sun) behind the Sun, W/S (and the up/down arrows) move the camera along the view direction at 80 AU/sec (Ovi, 2026-11-15), so that the long absolute space can be crossed; A/D, PgUp/PgDown and the mouse wheel keep the speeds above (before views_fix v_3.14 the mouse wheel moved the camera along the Sun's path at 100 AU/sec, which also took it out of the ecliptic plane).
	[REQ-03.0.15] In "celestial body reference system" mode, the meridian and parallel movements (see REQ-03.2) are done with a constant angular speed of 30 degrees/sec along the camera circle, regardless of the distance to the celestial body; in the tilted meridian view and in the pole view the E/C keys move the camera along their circle with the same angular speed. Where something seen would turn faster than 30 degrees/sec, the camera moves slower (see REQ-03.2.21; Ovi, 2026-11-17).

REQ-03.1
Camera control in "absolute space reference system" mode

	[REQ-03.1.1] In this mode the camera moves through absolute space only through the user's commands; it does not depend on the movement of the celestial bodies, except for the push described at the end of this requirement. In this mode a little cross is displayed in the middle of the screen, in order to be more clear where the camera view points. The cross is made of 4 segments (up, down, left and right) with a little empty space in the middle, so that the exact point the camera looks at remains visible (segments 8 pixels long and 2 pixels thick, empty space of 6 pixels, white with a thin dark outline so that it is visible on light and dark backgrounds).

	[REQ-03.1.2] This mode follows the common principles (see REQ-00, automatic and manual control) with rules of its own: there is no celestial body to look at, so the automatic view is the leveled head itself (its pan axis the z axis of the ecliptic, the picture always upright) and the mouse gives the manual look by turning that head; the keys and the mouse wheel move the camera. The "W"/"S" keys move it along the view direction ("fly where you look"), while the mouse wheel moves it along the pan vector (+x_cam, parallel to the ecliptic): both are rules of this mode.

	[REQ-03.1.3] In this case the camera is controlled as follows:
   - [REQ-03.1.4] by pressing the "up" arrow key or "W" the camera moves forward along the depth axis ("dolly move"). By pressing the "down" arrow key or "S" the camera moves backward.
   - [REQ-03.1.5] by rolling the mouse wheel forward the camera moves forward parallel to the ecliptic plane, along the pan vector (the direction the head faces, see REQ-00), whatever the tilt. By rolling the mouse wheel backward the camera moves backward the same way. The view direction ("pan" and "tilt" angles) does not change. Together with the "A"/"D" keys (left/right, see below) the wheel moves the camera in all directions parallel to the ecliptic plane. More than 60 AU behind the Sun the wheel keeps its speed (only W/S are faster there, see REQ-03.0.14).
   - [REQ-03.1.6] by moving the mouse forward and backward, the "tilt" angle of the camera changes. In this mode the tilt angle can only be changed between -90 and +90 degrees (the picture is always upright). If the tilt angle is -90 degrees and the user still moves the mouse forward nothing happens. Similarly if the tilt angle is +90 degrees and the user moves the mouse backward nothing happens.
When the mouse is moved forward, the view direction goes "down" (towards the bottom edge of the screen), and when it is moved backward, the view direction goes "up" (towards the top edge of the screen).
   - [REQ-03.1.7] by moving the mouse left and right, the "pan" angle of the camera changes. When the mouse is moved to the right, the view direction moves towards the left edge of the screen; when it is moved to the left, the view direction moves towards the right edge of the screen. Together with the forward and backward movement, the image follows the mouse, as if the user grabbed the image and pulled it ("grab the image").
   - [REQ-03.1.8] by pressing the "D" or "right arrow" keys the camera moves to the right, and by pressing the "A" or "left arrow" keys the camera moves to the left (a "truck" type of movement, also called "strafe" in games). The movement is parallel to the ecliptic plane and perpendicular to the view direction; the view direction ("pan" and "tilt" angles) does not change.
   - [REQ-03.1.9] by pressing the "PgUp" or "PgDown" keys, the camera moves away from/closer to the ecliptic plane (a pedestal/crane type of movement is performed), depending on the position of the camera (above or below the ecliptic plane). With the "PgUp" key the camera moves "up" (towards the top of the screen). With the "PgDown" key the camera moves "down" (towards the bottom of the screen).

	[REQ-03.1.10] The camera never comes closer than 2*R to the center of a celestial body (R is the radius of that body; the same minimum as in "celestial body reference system" mode). The planets and the Moon move, so a body can come to a camera that stands still (for example a camera placed on the orbit of a planet); in this case the body pushes the camera away:
   - [REQ-03.1.11] when the body passes the camera closer than 2*R, the camera is pushed sideways, perpendicular to the path of the body, so that it ends 2*R away from that path, on the side where it was. The body passes by and the camera stays next to the orbit. This is where the camera would get if it slid around the side of the body; on the time scale of the simulation a body moves much more than 2*R between two pictures on the screen (Pluto about 500 000 km, Earth about 3 million km at 3 months/sec), so the passing is checked along the whole path of the body between two pictures, not only at its new position.
   - [REQ-03.1.12] when the camera is exactly on the path of the body, "sideways" is not defined: the camera is pushed parallel to the ecliptic plane, in the direction opposite to the Sun (seen from the camera's position).
   - [REQ-03.1.13] when the body stops closer than 2*R before reaching the camera (it has not passed it yet), the camera is pushed straight away from the center of the body, to 2*R; if the camera is (almost) straight in front of the body on its orbit, it is pushed instead parallel to the ecliptic plane, in the direction opposite to the Sun, so that it is not carried along in front of the body.
The Sun does not move in the coordinate system from the simulation, so it never pushes the camera; it only stops the camera at 2*R from its center.

REQ-03.2
Camera control in "celestial body reference system" mode

	[REQ-03.2.1] In this mode the camera moves around the chosen celestial body (the reference celestial body, chosen in the menu, see REQ-01.2) through the user's commands.
	[REQ-03.2.2] In this case the camera's reference system is the center of the celestial body chosen by the user. The camera travels together with the celestial body along its orbit: its position relative to the center of the celestial body and its view direction stay the same, so the celestial body stays still on the screen while the stars, the grid and the other celestial bodies move. If the camera reaches the wall of the absolute space (the cylinder, see REQ-01) it stops there, and the celestial body moves away from it. The other celestial bodies push the camera away as in "absolute space reference system" mode (see REQ-03.1).

	[REQ-03.2.3] In this case the camera is controlled as follows:
   - [REQ-03.2.4] by rolling the mouse wheel forward and backward, the camera's reference system moves closer to or farther from the center of the chosen celestial body. The minimum distance of the camera from the center of the celestial body is 2*R (where R is the radius of the celestial body). In order to have the celestial body visible from big distances, the apparent radius of the celestial body (the angle under which its radius is seen from the camera) should not decrease below the apparent radius of the celestial body seen from a distance of 400 000 km (for Pluto about 0.17 degrees). For the Moon the minimum is half of the planets' (about 0.085 degrees; Ovi, 2026-10-30, it was 80%; this text corrected 2026-11-17); for the Sun it is twice as big as the planets', the apparent radius of Pluto seen from 200 000 km (about 0.34 degrees), so that from big distances the Sun is always the biggest dot ("who's the boss").
These values were adjusted after seeing them on the screen in the test simulation. This behavior is not real but for simulation purposes is very good. The planets all have the same minimum, so from big distances they are seen as dots of the same size. Besides, closer than 400 AU behind the Sun no celestial body is drawn smaller than a dot of 4 pixels across (the larger of the two counts); with a usual screen the minimums above are larger, except the Moon's: in a window less than about 1 180 pixels high the Moon is drawn as this dot. Far away (more than 400 AU behind the Sun, see REQ-02) there is no minimum and no smallest dot: the celestial bodies are drawn at their real size. The Earth and the Moon, drawn larger than real, never touch each other (else far away the drawn Earth takes the Moon in): the Earth is drawn at most with 60% and the Moon with 35% of the real distance between their centers, but never smaller than real nor smaller than their usual minimum (the larger minimums of the Moon locked and Earth locked views give way). Far away the real orbit of the Moon is much smaller than these drawn discs (from Pluto about 0.004 degrees, against at least 0.17 degrees). In the picture the distance of the Moon from the Earth is always the real one (Ovi, 2026-10-30); where the two discs overlap (farther than about 0.6 AU from the Earth) the one whose real center is nearer to the camera is drawn whole, in front of the other: when the Moon is behind the Earth, of the Moon only what the Earth leaves visible is seen. In the zoom screen (see REQ-01) the distance of the Moon from the Earth is drawn enlarged where needed, in its real direction, to 1.2 times the two drawn radii together, at every distance and every zoom (Ovi, 2026-10-30: a small gap between the discs; from Pluto about 50 times the real distance), so that the Moon is seen going around the Earth as Mercury around the Sun: out from behind the Earth, around it and in front of it (Ovi, 2026-10-30). The orbit line of the Moon is drawn in the zoom screen enlarged in the same way, and so is the trail of the Moon (its width and the length of its loops, see REQ-02), so that it starts from the drawn Moon. This is only the drawing: the path of the Moon stays real.
   - [REQ-03.2.5] by moving the mouse forward and backward, the "tilt" angle of the camera changes the same way as in "absolute space mode". When the mouse is moved forward, the view direction goes "down" (towards the bottom edge of the screen), and when it is moved backward, the view direction goes "up" (towards the top edge of the screen).
In the tilted meridian view (body center reference) the mouse gives the manual look, see "Tilted meridian view" below.
   - [REQ-03.2.6] by moving the mouse left and right, the "pan" angle of the camera changes. When the mouse is moved to the right, the view direction moves towards the left edge of the screen; when it is moved to the left, the view direction moves towards the right edge of the screen. Together with the forward and backward movement, the image follows the mouse, as if the user grabbed the image and pulled it ("grab the image").

   - [REQ-03.2.7] by pressing the "up arrow" or "W" keys, the camera circle (the circle around the center of the reference celestial body on which the camera moves) is tilted up, its C angle grows; by pressing the "down arrow" or "S" keys it is tilted down; the camera rides on it. The "E" and "C" keys walk the camera on the circle, "forward" (towards the top of the picture) and "backward" (Ovi, 2026-11-17: corrected; it said that W/S walk the camera on the circle, as in an older version).
This is called in this simulation "meridian movement". There are three meridian movements, chosen in the menu (see REQ-01.2), listed in this order: the "tilted meridian view", the "pole view" and the "Sun locked view"; on the Earth the "Moon locked view" comes after the Sun locked view, on the Moon the "Earth locked view" (see below); on the Sun there is no Sun locked view (choosing the Sun from the Sun locked view gives the tilted meridian view, on the real meridian). The program starts with the Sun locked view (see REQ-03). Choosing a view sets the tilted meridian back to the real meridian (C angle 90 degrees).

[REQ-03.2.8]
Tilted meridian view ("Tilted meridian"):
This view is built like the pole view (see REQ-00, automatic and manual control): the keys move the camera on the tilted meridian and set the head and the automatic view; the mouse adds the manual look. The head is that of the body center reference (see REQ-00): its pan axis (+z_cam) is the camera radius, away from the celestial body, and the camera, tilted -90 degrees in the head, looks along it at the center of the celestial body (the automatic view). The pan vector of the automatic view (the up of the automatic picture) is the way "E" walks on the tilted meridian, so that the tilted meridian always runs up and down the automatic picture. At a C angle of 90 degrees (the real meridian) it is the classic view on latitude and longitude, known by everyone (the meridian view of the earlier versions, removed in v_3.2: "W/S" walked there as "E/C" do here): the pole ahead is in the upper part of the screen; from the Home position the north pole is at the top, and when the camera passes over a pole, that pole is then in the lower part of the screen, "E" still walking up the screen. The head is fixed to the axes (Ovi, 2026-11-25), as that of the polar pan (REQ-03.2.24): its +x is the rotation axis (towards the north pole) projected on the plane of the head, +y = z x x; the pan vector (the way "E" walks) turns in this head. Only the head changes (its axes, and the head pan and head tilt in the camera log): the picture and the moves are the same.
[REQ-03.2.9] In the Home position the camera looks at the center of the celestial body, north up, with no manual look, and the tilted meridian is the real meridian (C angle 90 degrees).
   - [REQ-03.2.10] E/C (the same keys as in the pole view): the camera walks on the tilted meridian, at 30 degrees/sec, "E" forward (+x_cam, towards the top of the automatic picture), "C" backward.
   - [REQ-03.2.11] W/S (as in the pole view, they tilt the circle): the tilted meridian turns around its line of nodes (the line where it crosses the equator) and the camera rides on it (it keeps its place on the circle), at 30 degrees/sec: "W" or "up arrow" raises the C angle up to 90 degrees (the real meridian), "S" or "down arrow" lowers it down to 0 degrees (the equator), and they stop there. The C angle of the tilted meridian has this full range from any place of the camera; the range is specific to this view (in the pole view the C angle of circle C goes from -80 to +80 degrees). Where both ways would change the C angle in the same way (at 0 degrees for "W", at 90 degrees for "S"), "W" takes the camera north and "S" goes back the way "W" came. The automatic picture is carried with the camera, the tilted meridian staying up and down the screen. Q and L have no function in this view.
   - [REQ-03.2.12] mouse: the manual look, as in the pole view: the image follows the mouse ("grab the image"); left/right turns the view around the up of the automatic picture, forward/backward then around the new right of the screen, at most 90 degrees up or down (the picture is never upside down). The look is kept through every movement (W/S, E/C, A/D, the mouse wheel), relative to the automatic view, so the celestial body keeps its place on the screen and can be anywhere on it, or off it, the view in the sky (the core requirement applies while the celestial body is in view, see REQ-00.0.24).
   - [REQ-03.2.13] A/D (as in the pole view): the tilted meridian turns around the rotation axis of the celestial body together with the camera, at 30 degrees/sec, so that its C angle and the place of the camera on it are kept: only its line of nodes turns, and the camera moves on its parallel of the celestial body. "D" towards the right edge of the automatic picture when the key is pressed, "A" the other way. Only W/S change the C angle.
	[REQ-03.2.14] Chosen in the menu, the view starts at its Home position, on the way to Home (see REQ-01.2 and REQ-03.2.41; Ovi, 2026-11-26; until then nothing changed on the screen entering the view: the manual look was taken from the camera as it was, and the picture turned to the automatic picture at 90 degrees/sec around the center of the celestial body, while the camera waited). The gauge shows the C angle of the tilted meridian, the camera's latitude to the equator (the green vector) and the camera's latitude and longitude, as in the pole view (see REQ-01.1); the head pan and head tilt of the view direction are recorded in the camera log (see REQ-05).

[REQ-03.2.15]
Pole view ("Pole view"):
This view is referred to the reference celestial body itself: its rotation axis and its equator (see REQ-00) take the place of the z axis and of the body ecliptic, so the movements of the camera go together with the spin of the celestial body. The ecliptic plane, the grid, the reference axes and the trails are then seen tilted on the screen; this is expected. The camera is always on circle C (see REQ-00). The view is made of the automatic view and the manual look (see REQ-00, automatic and manual control): the keys move the camera on circle C and set the head and the automatic view, which looks at the center of the celestial body; the mouse adds the manual look.
   - [REQ-03.2.16] W/S: they only change the C angle, "W" or "up arrow" up, "S" or "down arrow" down, at 30 degrees/sec, between -80 and +80 degrees (passing through 0 degrees): circle C turns around its line of nodes and the camera rides on it (it keeps its place on C). At the highest or the lowest point of C the camera moves north or south together with the C angle; at a node it does not move, only C tilts under it. This view is referred to the body ecliptic (the ecliptic plane through the center of the reference celestial body), not to the equator (see REQ-00). Its purpose is to show the celestial body and the Sun together, the celestial body lit by it, both moving automatically: while the simulation runs the celestial body keeps its place on the screen and the Sun keeps its direction sideways; the changes of the Sun's height are real and are seen. It is built on the leveled pan of the pole view (see "Pole view" above: circle C, E/C, A/D, the mouse wheel), with its reference changed and with rules of its own (Ovi, 2026-11-11; reworked by Ovi 2026-11-25: circle C at a fixed angle, the picture never upside down, no gauge):
   - [REQ-03.2.28] reference system: centered on the celestial body, its z axis that of the ecliptic, its x axis towards the Sun (in the ecliptic plane); the body ecliptic takes the place of the equator and the z axis the place of the rotation axis: circle C is at the C angle to the body ecliptic, its highest point is its point farthest north of the body ecliptic, and the poles of the view are the ecliptic poles.

   - [REQ-03.2.41] by pressing the "Home" key, the camera returns to a known (predefined) position and view direction, relative to the reference celestial body and to the body ecliptic: the Home position.

   2. the path: a straight segment from the camera's place to the Home position; its start stays with the celestial body it leaves (in space's axes), its end with the new one (in the frame of its view). The camera leaves the celestial body it starts from slowly, its distance from it growing gradually on the scale of that distance, then faster and faster, and comes to the new one the same way reversed, on the scale of the Home distance; in the middle of a long way it is very fast. So the celestial bodies drift on the screen at the beginning and at the end of the way. On the way the camera keeps at least 2 radii from every celestial body, as in every move (see REQ-03.2.2).
   3. the pace: the camera's way and the view's turn go together, from rest, fastest in the middle (2.19 times their mean speed) and back to rest: the share of the way s = -20x^7 + 70x^6 - 84x^5 + 35x^4 of the share of the time x (Ovi, 2026-11-26: slow near the celestial bodies, fast in the travel). The move takes 2 seconds plus 2 seconds times the length of the segment / 40 AU, at most 4 seconds: 2 seconds near a celestial body, about 4 seconds from Pluto to the Sun (Ovi, 2026-11-26).
The sizes a view draws (the Earth and the Moon in the Moon locked and Earth locked views, see above) go over to those of the new view gradually, on a logarithmic scale: along the way to Home, or in 3 seconds when the view changes without one (Ovi, 2026-11-22: they changed at once; since 2026-11-26 every choice of a view in the menu has one, see REQ-01.2).
Nothing stops it (Ovi, 2026-11-19: before, any camera control stopped it where it was, the camera left somewhere in space): the keys, the mouse and the wheel do nothing until it has ended (a key still held then moves the camera from Home); the Home key starts it again, and choosing another view or celestial body in the menu starts a new one. The tilted meridian is set back to the real meridian (C angle 90 degrees).

REQ-04
Stars, constellations and the Milky Way

	[REQ-04.0.1] Real constellations are visible from the simulation space: Ursa Major, Ursa Minor, Cassiopeia, Cygnus, Lyra, Pegasus, Andromeda, Auriga, Taurus, Crux, Carina, Centaurus, Octans, Hercules and Columba, and the constellations of the stars from which the motion of the Sun was determined (see below): Canis Major, Gemini, Canis Minor, Leo, Bootes, Aquila, Sagittarius and Vela, and, so that the sky south of the ecliptic is covered as well, Cetus, Dorado, Lupus, Triangulum Australe, Grus and Tucana, and Orion, Scorpius, Virgo and Aquarius (Ovi, 2026-11-02) (the list may be extended later).
Some of them are related to the motion of the Sun through space, which was determined from the light of the stars (their positions over the years and the Doppler shift in their spectra): Hercules contains the solar apex, the point of the sky towards which the Sun moves relative to the nearby stars (not far from Vega, in Lyra); Columba contains the solar antapex, the point the Sun moves away from; Cygnus is in the direction of the Sun's motion around the center of the galaxy. The Sun moves towards Cygnus at about 230 km/s (see REQ-02). Herschel found the solar apex in 1783 from the motions on the sky of seven bright stars: Sirius (Canis Major), Castor and Pollux (Gemini), Procyon (Canis Minor), Regulus (Leo), Arcturus (Bootes) and Altair (Aquila). Sagittarius contains the center of the galaxy (Sgr A*): its motion on the sky gives the speed of the Sun around the center of the galaxy, about 230 km/s. Vela contains the point the Sun moves away from around the galaxy (galactic longitude 270 degrees), opposite Cygnus.
[REQ-04.0.2] Their stars are drawn in their real directions on the sky (real right ascension and declination, converted to the ecliptic coordinates of the simulation). The stars are taken from the Hipparcos catalogue (J2000): the stars of the constellation lines, the stars with a name label and the other stars of these constellations down to magnitude 4.5. The stars are so far away that their directions are the same from every point of the absolute space: they do not move when the camera moves through space, only when the view direction changes (this is a very good approximation: from the far end of the absolute space, 8 000 AU behind the Sun, the nearest stars would really move by up to about 1.7 degrees, Alpha Centauri; around the planets less than 0.01 degrees). They are drawn behind everything else (bodies, orbits, trails, axes). Every constellation is drawn with faint lines, 1 pixel wide, between its stars (the usual figure of the constellation), under the stars; a line stops 2 pixels before the glow of each of its stars, and in this gap the sky behind is seen (black, or the Milky Way; nothing is drawn around the star), so that the figure stays easy to read also in front of the Milky Way, at any zoom and in the zoom screen.
[REQ-04.0.3] The brightness of every star corresponds to its real apparent magnitude, but a star that has a name label is always drawn at least at a minimum visible brightness, so that it can be seen even if it is faint in reality (for example Sigma Octantis, magnitude about 5.4). The color of every star corresponds to its real color (its B-V index), from blue-white to orange.

	[REQ-04.0.4] The Milky Way: the band of light of our galaxy is drawn behind the stars, as seen from the Earth on a dark night. It is the glow of the galaxy's disk without its stars, from a real map: NASA's Deep Star Maps 2020 (NASA Goddard Space Flight Center, Scientific Visualization Studio, made from the Hipparcos, Tycho and Gaia star catalogues), in its smallest size (1024 x 512 pixels), so that the simulation's files stay small. The map is placed in real right ascension and declination (J2000), as the stars: the brightest part is towards Sagittarius, the center of the galaxy (Sagittarius A*, RA 17h 45m 40s, Dec -29 deg 00'), the dark dust lanes (the Great Rift from Cygnus to Sagittarius) are in their real places, and the band passes through Cygnus and Vela, the directions in which the Sun moves around the center of the galaxy and from which it comes. It is dimmed so that the constellations remain easy to read; in the zoom screen it is dimmed more, to 20% of its brightness on the main screen; like the stars, it does not move when the camera moves through space. The "H" key does not hide it (it is part of the Universe, see REQ-01).

	[REQ-04.0.9] The stellar aberration (found by James Bradley in 1728; Ovi, 2026-11-20): the Earth moves across the light of the stars, so every star is seen shifted towards the direction of the Earth's motion. The simulation shows it magnified (see REQ-01.1.8): a star whose catalogue direction is the unit vector u is drawn in the direction u + k*v/c, normalized, where v is the velocity of the Earth relative to the Sun at the simulated date (on its orbit, about 29.3 to 30.3 km/s), c the speed of light (299 792.458 km/s) and k the magnification: a shift of about k*(v/c)*sin(theta) (at most about 20.5 arc seconds x k), theta being the angle between the star and the direction of the Earth's motion. Only the Earth's motion on its orbit counts: the shift caused by the Sun's motion around the galaxy has stayed the same for millions of years and is already in the catalogue places. All the stars move (the stars of the constellations and the background stars), and with them the constellation lines, their names and the Milky Way. Over a year, every star goes once around a small path centred on its catalogue place: a short line for the stars on the ecliptic, a circle for those at the ecliptic poles, an ellipse in between (its width / length = the sine of the star's ecliptic latitude). When it is off, the stars are at their catalogue places.
	[REQ-04.0.10] The stellar parallax and the proper motion (Ovi, 2026-11-20 - 23), shown magnified (see REQ-01.1.28) for 16 near stars, with their parallax p and proper motion (mu_alpha*, mu_delta) from the Hipparcos catalogue, new reduction (F. van Leeuwen, 2007): alpha Cen B (alpha Cen A, 11 arc seconds away, moves with it, with B's values), Sirius, Procyon, Tau Ceti, Altair and Arcturus (large parallax); 82 Eridani, HD 136352 and Iota Persei (a negative Copernican theory supporters' parallax, see REQ-04.0.11); 61 Cygni A (the first parallax measured, Bessel 1838); Sigma and Chi Draconis, Beta Hydri and Zeta2 Reticuli (near the ecliptic's poles); Xi Bootis and Zeta Doradus (moving almost with the Sun: a small proper motion and a small radial velocity, Gaia DR3). Each of them has a name label. The parallax: a star whose catalogue direction is u is seen from the Earth, not from the Sun: in the direction u - k*p*E (normalized), E the Earth's place relative to the Sun (AU) at the simulated date, k the magnification; over a year it goes once around an ellipse centred on its catalogue place: a short line for a star on the ecliptic, a circle at the ecliptic's poles. The proper motion (the star's own motion across the sky, relative to the Sun): the star stays at its catalogue place and swings around it with its parallax; its proper motion is seen in its yellow trail, which stays behind it: the place t years ago, relative to the star now, u - k*mu*t - k*p*E(now - t). The stars within 10 degrees of the view direction (at most 4) get their trail (3 years back, not before the moment the parallax was switched on or the time changed its direction, fading; none while the time goes backward, as the trails of the celestial bodies), the line of their proper motion and a dot where the trail crosses it. All the other stars stay at their catalogue places: the background against which these move. The trails, the lines and the dots get the aberration of today (see REQ-04.0.9), as the background stars around them. When it is off, all the stars are at their catalogue places.
	[REQ-04.0.11] The Copernican theory supporters' parallax (Ovi, 2026-11-22; Copernicus: the Sun, and so all the stars, fixed; not real, stated in About): the star's angle measured only against the background stars, its proper motion not taken out, between the Earth at +x-ecl and, about 178.8 days later, at -x-ecl (the real orbit), read as the parallax a fixed star would need for that shift: p' = p + dt*(mu.w)/(w.w), w = E1 - E2 across the line of sight (AU), dt the time between E1 and E2. Positive: the star moves towards +x-ecl, opposite to the Earth, as a fixed star would; negative: the other way (possible only with a proper motion). The catalogue parallax p (Bessel 1838, Hipparcos) is the yearly ellipse fitted beside the proper motion, positive for every star.
	[REQ-04.0.12] The star names (Ovi, 2026-11-23): a star's name is placed beside its star once (see REQ-04.0.8), and that place is kept on the screen, in pixels, in every view (also where the picture is turned), so the name stays attached to its star. While the parallax is shown, the name of a star with its own motion is fixed to its catalogue place, outside the band its trail can ever cover (3 years of proper motion widened by its whole parallax ellipse), clear of the other such stars' bands and names: ahead of the star (the way it moves on the screen), or, when another trail pushes it more than 16 pixels farther, on the nearest free side; it does not move while the time runs, and a change of its place (the view turned) glides in about 0.15 s.

IMPL-01
Implementation note: precision of large coordinates ("floating origin").

The absolute space reaches 8 050 AU from the Sun. Graphics cards compute with 32-bit numbers, which at 8 050 AU can store positions only in steps of about 130 000 km (much more than the radius of the Earth, about 6 400 km, and of Pluto, about 1 200 km). Therefore everything drawn on the screen (celestial bodies, trails, orbits, grid, axes and other long lines) is computed relative to the camera or to a reference point near it, not in absolute coordinates. Only far away objects keep large coordinates; for them the error is much smaller than one pixel.
   - long straight lines (axes, grid lines, the outline and the axis of the cylinder) are rebuilt around the camera every frame: short pieces near the camera and longer pieces (doubling in length) farther away, as in the test simulation.
   - trails and orbits are drawn relative to the camera, as in the test simulation. An orbit line is built again only when needed (Ovi, 2026-11-14; before views_fix v_3.13 at every picture): its points are kept relative to the place of the camera when it was built, exact enough while the camera has moved less than half its distance to the line (the rounding of a point stays far below a pixel); it is built again when the camera has moved farther, while the camera is near the line and, for the orbit of the Moon, when its points change (the time, the enlargement of the Moon's distance).

Architecture: ARC-P-06; updateLines, updateTrail, updateOrbitLine, moonLinePoints, frame

IMPL-02
Implementation note: drawing the far parts of the trails.

Far away a block of trail points has segments much shorter than a pixel, and all of them would be drawn (Ovi, 2026-11-14: Chrome became slow while the ends of the trails were in the picture; Pluto, Sun locked view, at 603 years: about 111 000 segments, 25 pictures per second). So a far block is drawn with only every 2nd, 4th ... 512th of its points (each of these versions made once, when first needed): the largest step for which its longest drawn segment stays within 2 pixels at its nearest point. The line on the screen is the same (the points left out are less than a pixel from it), with far fewer segments (there: 50 pictures per second). The zoom screen chooses for its own, smaller pixels.

IMPL-03
Implementation note: the same picture with every three.js version (see REQ-01.0.33).

Between three.js 0.160 and 0.187 two changes of three.js altered the picture of the simulation (found 2026-11-15, Ovi: the constellation lines in the zoom screen were weaker in Chrome with 0.187); the simulation now sets both itself (views_fix v_3.17):
   - the wide lines (the constellation lines, the trails: LineSegments2 and LineMaterial, add-ons of three.js) measure their width in the pixels of the picture they are drawn in (their resolution).    - the see-through objects (the Sun's glow, the orbit lines, the grid, the trails) are blended from the farthest to the nearest. three.js 0.160 measured their distance after the perspective division, which turned the order over for the objects whose center is behind the camera (the long lines built around the camera); from 0.166 it is measured before the division. The simulation sorts them itself, by their true distance along the view, the same with every version.
Compared on 11 scenes (views_fix v_3.17): 0.160 and 0.187 then draw the same pictures, at most 9 of 255 brightness levels apart, in the plumes (a soft gradient computed by the simulation's own shader). From views_fix v_3.18 the simulation ships 0.187.0.
